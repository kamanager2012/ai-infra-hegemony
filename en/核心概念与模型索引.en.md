# Core Concepts and Model Index

> This page defines the common analytical vocabulary used across the report. The goal is not to manufacture jargon, but to stop the same mechanism from being renamed, duplicated, or misread as an already-observed fact.

> **Vocabulary policy:** this is not a catalog of proprietary theories. Terms in this index serve three different roles:
> - **report-defined analytical constructs:** Incremental Scarce-Capacity Access, Non-AI Residual Capacity, Rolling Bottleneck Cascade, cloud system-stack substitution + parallel-stack closure boundary, Financing Reflexivity, AI Capacity Gap / Growth Gap, and AI inheritance;
> - **descriptive shorthand:** resource reallocation pressure, Super-buyer Effect, scarce-layer value capture, AI Price-Tolerance Gap, two-speed semiconductor economics, anchor-buyer mechanism, and Three-Layer AI Skills Stack;
> - **standard or imported analytical vocabulary:** crowding-out, capacity preemption, compute rent, installed-base flywheel, hysteresis, unit economics, task-to-job translation, and foreclosure pressure.
>
> Only the first group is intended to carry report-specific model structure. The other terms are navigation labels or standard analytical language and should not be cited as independent discoveries.

## 1. Resource Reallocation Pressure

**Definition:** when AI/HPC demand combines higher willingness to pay, stronger order certainty, longer contracts, and higher expected returns, scarce resources—advanced logic, HBM, advanced packaging, power, land, talent, and capital—tend to be allocated toward AI/HPC.

**Type:** Mechanism (M), not a market-share statistic.

**Failure conditions:** supply grows materially faster than AI absorption; AI returns fall; or technology reduces dependence on the same scarce resources.

## 2. Super-buyer Effect

**Definition:** very large AI platforms gain disproportionate influence over resource allocation through order scale, prepayments, long-duration commitments, credit quality, and roadmap certainty.

This is not “highest bidder takes all capacity.” It is a multi-variable ranking of long-term economic value to suppliers.

## 3. Incremental Scarce-Capacity Access

**Definition:** evaluate whether different buyers can repeatedly obtain sufficient, qualified, and timely supply in the next generation of scarce physical resources.

Analyze each physical layer separately:

- advanced logic;
- HBM / DRAM;
- advanced packaging;
- networking;
- land / power / shell;
- energized power.

The report does not compress these physical pools into one synthetic cross-layer percentage.

More reliable variables include:

- qualification status;
- lead time;
- long-term agreement / prepayment;
- allocation fulfillment;
- supplier diversification;
- delivery schedule;
- buyer credit quality;
- supply elasticity.

**Boundary:** revenue share, demand share, wafer share, packaging allocation, IT-GW, and generation GW are not interchangeable.

## 4. Non-AI Residual Capacity

```text
NonAI_Residual(t)
= Effective_Supply(t)
- AI_Absorption(t)
- Other_Priority_Demand(t)
```

Used to test whether capacity expansion actually restores supply to smartphones, PCs, general servers, automotive, and other non-AI industries.

## 5. Observed Crowding-out

Non-AI industries receive fewer effective resources in absolute terms, or visibly face lower specifications, delays, or materially higher prices.

This can be observed directly.

## 6. Counterfactual Crowding-out

Non-AI supply still grows, but less than it would have grown without rapid AI absorption.

This explains how “total capacity expanded” and “traditional industries still feel constrained” can both be true.

## 7. Capacity Preemption

**Definition:** reserving future capacity through prepayments, long-term agreements, capacity commitments, or first-launch scheduling.

Capacity preemption is not automatically a legally exclusive agreement. The report distinguishes:
- disclosed aggregate commitments;
- supplier-specific commitments;
- node/packaging-specific commitments;
- report-side forward inference.

## 8. Scarcity-layer Value Capture

Replaces the older phrase “margin monopoly.”

**Definition:** firms operating in scarce, difficult-to-substitute layers can earn excess profit, cash flow, and bargaining power, then reinvest those gains into the next generation of resource reservation.

High gross margin is one indicator. It is not a legal monopoly finding.

## 9. Compute Rent

**Definition:** excess value capture arising from scarcity in compute platforms, software ecosystems, hardware supply, and system integration.

Boundary:
- NVIDIA Data Center revenue is an important seller-side proxy for AI-infrastructure monetary flow;
- it is not equal to “all global compute rent.”

## 10. Installed-base Flywheel

```text
more resources
→ more shipments
→ more installations
→ more developer/customer feedback
→ stronger software and next-generation products
→ stronger cash flow and order certainty
→ stronger prepayment capacity
→ more resources in the next generation
```

This is a core dynamic in the report's system-concentration scenarios.

## 11. Rolling Bottleneck Cascade

The scarce layer does not have to remain the same for ten years.

Bottlenecks may move across:
- advanced logic;
- HBM;
- advanced packaging;
- substrates;
- networking;
- liquid cooling;
- main transformers;
- grid interconnection;
- generation capacity and long-duration power contracts.

Even if one component loosens, a downstream system can remain constrained if the bottleneck rolls to the next layer.

## 12. AI Price-Tolerance Gap

**Definition:** AI-infrastructure buyers can often tolerate a higher unit resource price and higher capital cost than many consumer-electronics or traditional-industrial buyers.

This helps explain why market-clearing allocation can tilt toward AI/HPC.

## 13. Hysteresis

Once fabs, HBM lines, packaging capacity, data centers, and power infrastructure are built around AI demand, they do not instantly revert to traditional consumer capacity if AI demand later cools.

Shortage, expansion, surplus, and recovery are therefore asymmetric processes.

## 14. Self-financing Demand Loop

A leading platform uses strong profit and free cash flow to fund expansion, prepayments, and supply commitments, allowing current market power to become future supply advantage.

## 15. Capacity Foreclosure Pressure

**Definition:** if a leading platform repeatedly obtains materially stronger quantity assurance and earlier delivery across multiple scarce layers, competitors may lose effective competitiveness through residual capacity, lead times, cost, and product-cycle delay even without being legally prohibited from buying.

This is an industrial-economics scenario concept, not a legal finding.

## 15A. Cloud system-stack substitution

**Definition:** hyperscalers do not merely replace one GPU die with one custom ASIC. They can build a system stack from chips, interconnect, compilers/runtimes, racks/pods, internal workloads, and data-center standards.

By 2026, Google Ironwood, AWS Trainium3 UltraServer, Microsoft Maia, and Meta MTIA show that system-level substitution is real.

Key questions:
- how much training and inference moves to these stacks;
- whether software migration is sustainable;
- whether they have credible scale-up / scale-out systems;
- whether dependence on NVIDIA rack-scale systems declines;
- whether they still depend on the same HBM, advanced logic, packaging, and power;
- whether internal cost advantages become external platform competition.

System-stack substitution affects **platform profit pools**. Whether those stacks avoid the same upstream resources affects **supply-chain competition**. These are separate effects.

## 15B. Anchor-buyer mechanism

**Definition:** when model companies make multi-year or decade-scale compute commitments, they become anchor customers for data-center, chip, power, and financing projects rather than merely cloud tenants.

Current anchors:
- OpenAI Stargate: 2025-01 announced a **US$500B** four-year infrastructure plan; by 2026-04 OpenAI said it had passed a **10 GW secured-capacity milestone**; the **1 GW** Michigan campus broke ground in 2026-06;
- PORTS-Pike / NVIDIA / SB Energy / OpenAI: initial **4.25 IT-GW** design capacity supported by land + power + shell (LPS) coordination and credit support, with phased delivery planned from 2028;
- Anthropic / AWS: more than **US$100B** over ten years, up to **5 GW**, with large-scale Trainium use.

Mechanism:

```text
long-duration model demand
→ greater infrastructure financing certainty
→ earlier cloud / data-center / power construction
→ more stable chip and memory orders
→ priority capacity for the model company
→ larger training and inference scale
→ stronger future demand commitments
```

This creates two-way dependency: platforms own heavy assets and physical infrastructure; model companies provide high-value demand and future utilization.

Boundary: contract value or GW commitment ≠ already energized load, chip price, actual utilization, or profit split.

## 15C. AI Software Unit Economics

AI-native software does not have near-zero marginal cost in the same way as traditional pure-software replication.

```text
AI_Gross_Margin
=
(Revenue
- Model_Inference_Cost
- Retrieval_Storage_Cost
- Tool_API_Cost
- Human_Review_Cost
- AI_Specific_Cloud_Cost)
/
Revenue
```

Owning GPUs or private racks only converts part of external API cost into depreciation, power, operations, and capital cost.

Therefore the old “SaaS 85% → AI SaaS 55%” endpoint is not an industry fact.

Key variables:
- ARPU / outcome pricing;
- calls per user;
- Agent loop depth;
- routing and caching;
- accelerator/cloud price;
- human review and tool/API cost;
- private-infrastructure utilization.

Lower gross margin does not automatically mean lower total profit if ARPU or market size rises.

## 15D. Task-to-Job Translation Layer

AI labor impact cannot jump directly from "task exposure" to "unemployment."

```text
Model Capability
→ Task Suitability
→ Workflow Redesign
→ Adoption Economics / Responsibility
→ Job Composition
→ Employment / Wage Outcome
```

- **Model Capability:** can the system technically perform the task?
- **Task Suitability:** is the task digital, evaluable, repeatable, and sufficiently structured?
- **Workflow Redesign:** did the organization actually reorganize work?
- **Adoption Economics / Responsibility:** do cost, quality, compliance, safety, and accountability permit substitution?
- **Job Composition:** which tasks inside a job are compressed, augmented, or retained?
- only then: employment, wage, and organizational outcomes.

ILO's 2025 refined index remains an empirical anchor: roughly one quarter of global jobs have some GenAI exposure, while the highest exposure category is around 3.3%; most jobs are more likely to be transformed than directly eliminated.

## 15E. Three-Layer AI Skills Stack

1. **Tool / cognitive layer:** model use, retrieval, evaluation, workflow design.
2. **Systems-engineering layer:** compilers, runtimes, multi-model scheduling, interconnect, racks, data pipelines, reliability, and cost.
3. **Physical / responsibility layer:** power, heat, packaging, yield, PPAs, sites, licenses, safety, and compliance.

The first layer scales fastest and is easiest for model capability itself to commoditize. The second governs production deployment and migration. The third expands slowest because it is constrained by physical systems and accountability.

## 15F. Two-Speed Semiconductor Economics

**Definition:** the AI Infrastructure Economy and the Traditional Device Economy share parts of the same upstream supply chain but differ materially in willingness to pay, long-duration contracts/prepayments, expected capital returns, BOM constraints, and end-market price elasticity. Incremental capital and effective supply can therefore be allocated to the two demand classes at different speeds.

The core proposition is not that consumer electronics disappear. It is:

> **AI/HPC is increasing the influence of a relatively small number of capital-intensive buyers over marginal semiconductor demand and incremental capacity formation.**

Observable variables include HPC / smartphone / PC revenue mix, advanced-process CapEx, HBM/server-memory product mix, advanced-packaging investment, consumer BOM/pricing, replacement cycles, and Non-AI Residual Capacity.

**Weakening conditions:** AI Capacity Gap converges, supply elasticity rises materially, traditional devices regain stable residual supply, or AI workload monetization/utilization fails to support the expanding capital base.

## 15G. AI Capacity Gap / AI Capacity Growth Gap

To avoid mislabeling a growth-rate difference as a stock capacity shortfall, the report separates two metrics.

### Absolute gap

```text
AI_Capacity_Gap_i(t)
= AI_Effective_Demand_i(t)
- AI_Effective_Capacity_i(t)
```

where \(i\) is measured separately for advanced logic, HBM/DRAM, advanced packaging, networking, power, and other physical layers. Demand and capacity must use the same delivery window, qualification/quality boundary, and physical unit.

**Purpose:** determine whether the layer is currently short of deliverable supply.

- **Absolute Gap > 0:** current shortage;
- **Absolute Gap ≈ 0:** near current balance;
- **Absolute Gap < 0:** current effective headroom.

### Growth gap

```text
AI_Capacity_Growth_Gap_i(t)
= Growth(AI_Effective_Demand_i(t))
- Growth(AI_Effective_Capacity_i(t))
```

The Growth Gap is a same-period difference in growth rates, measured in percentage points. It indicates whether the absolute gap is more likely to widen or converge.

- **Growth Gap > 0:** scarcity may widen, or existing headroom may erode;
- **Growth Gap < 0:** scarcity may converge, or headroom may expand;
- **Growth Gap = 0:** does not imply that no current shortage exists.

A strong S4 signal requires the Absolute Gap to turn negative or effective excess capacity to emerge while utilization, clearing prices, forward commitments, accounting indicators, and demand-financing quality deteriorate together.

AI Capacity Gap / Growth Gap are complementary to Non-AI Residual Capacity: the first two describe the AI-side current gap and its direction; NARC describes effective supply left for non-AI demand after priority allocation.

## 15H. Financing Reflexivity

**Definition:** when a system seller also uses equity investment, credit enhancement, LPS support, or compute-financing coordination to help customers and infrastructure projects obtain capital, seller-side demand growth and customer financing conditions can become mutually reinforcing.

A representative loop is:

```text
platform cash generation
→ equity / credit support
→ infrastructure financing improves
→ anchor capacity expands
→ platform-system demand expands
```

This does not mean demand is artificial.

The report distinguishes:

1. independently financed end demand;
2. long-duration Anchor Buyer commitments;
3. third-party infrastructure capital;
4. supplier equity / credit support.

If utilization and monetization are healthy, supplier-assisted financing can accelerate real infrastructure formation.

If utilization weakens while supplier support and refinancing dependency keep rising, the independence of forward-demand signals should be downgraded.


## 15I. AI inheritance

**Definition:** AI does not need to rebuild the digital economy from zero. It can inherit decades of Information Technology infrastructure—software, databases, cloud, APIs, digital documents, source code, identity/access systems, MES/ERP/CRM, and networked workflows—and add understanding, generation, prediction, decision, and execution layers on top.

**Type:** Mechanism (M), not evidence that every industry is already AI-native.

Core transmission:

```text
Digital Installed Base
→ AI Overlay
→ Workflow Penetration
→ Automation Depth
→ Workload Volume
→ Infrastructure Demand
```

**Long-run implication:** AI-infrastructure demand depends not only on frontier-model scaling, but also on how deeply AI penetrates existing digital workflows, how frequently those workflows execute, and how much automation depth they acquire. Algorithmic efficiency can reduce resources per task while total infrastructure demand still rises if workflow penetration or execution volume grows faster.

**Boundary:**
- the marginal friction of Digital → AI can be lower than Industrial → Digital, but it is not zero;
- production adoption still depends on data quality, integration, security, governance, responsibility, reliability, human review, and unit economics;
- insufficiently digitized Physical AI / industrial processes still require sensorization, control integration, qualification, safety, and field modification;
- a digital installed base represents potential convertible workload, not realized utilization, revenue, or cash flow.

## 16. Three quantities that must remain separate

1. **Total supply** — total market capacity / bit / wafer / output.
2. **AI absorption** — resources actually consumed by AI/HPC.
3. **Non-AI residual** — effective resources available to other industries after priority allocation.

Any chapter that reports only the first quantity and concludes “supply has recovered” is incomplete.

## 17. Relationship among the models

```text
Resource reallocation pressure
        ↓
Super-buyer effect / anchor-buyer mechanism
        ↓
Capacity Preemption / Financing Support
        ↓
Layer-specific Incremental Scarce-Capacity Access strengthens
        ↓
Non-AI Residual Capacity / competitor access changes
        ↓
Installed-base Flywheel
        ↓
Scarcity-layer Value Capture
        ↓
Self-financing Demand Loop / Financing Reflexivity
        ↺
```

Rolling Bottlenecks in power, packaging, HBM, and other layers determine where this loop temporarily slows, migrates, or reverses.

## 18. Research discipline

- Facts describe what has happened.
- Hypotheses use “if,” assumptions, and stress tests.
- Models define variables and causal transmission.
- Scenarios may be bold.
- Legal terminology is used only with legal support.
- Every strong claim should name at least one reverse signal.
