## 1. The question this volume answers

The draft Volume 8 asked: if the four clouds spent $215B of capex in 2024, and hardware is depreciated over four years, will that throw off $45B to $55B of hard cost each year from 2025 through 2028, and whose cash flow cracks first? On the judgment, that question welds two claims, and both fail. First, $215B is not an addable sum for four firms on the same calendar year, the same line item, and still less an AI-hardware split. Second, the $45B to $55B that falls out is a four-year straight-line assumption, not AI-server depreciation the four firms disclosed. This volume asks three other things.

First, can group-wide equipment purchases on the buyer side cover a 71% to 75% gross margin on selling cabinets? If they cannot, the bid is still the seller's. Second, where does the cash in prepayments and supply commitments come from? The judgment is explicit: NVIDIA's hundred billion is earned selling the scarce slice itself; prepayments are reproduction. Microsoft's hundred billion builds halls. Third, can antitrust and a fine change the rules overnight? The inquiries are real. Complaints and fines have not landed. 10% is a statutory ceiling, not a number about to be issued. The White House, Commerce, and DoD have already written advanced chips and large compute clusters as national security. What can interrupt the line is a split or a forced-open unified compute architecture, not the ceiling times revenue.

This volume does only the capital side and the rule side. It does not tear down a single fab line. It does not report unverified cabinet counts. It does not knock mixed-year capex against data-center revenue into a rent rate. The numbers are current. Sentences that push forward from those numbers are marked as analysis added here. They are not promoted to filing sentences that already happened.

### 2.5 National security: advanced compute is already written as national security

White House, 24 October 2024, the first National Security Memorandum on artificial intelligence. The fact sheet writes: advances at the frontier of AI will have significant implications for national security and foreign policy; developing advanced systems requires large volumes of advanced chips; the memorandum directs actions to improve the security and diversity of chip supply chains. The body is National Security Memorandum/NSM-25. Commerce BIS, 7 October 2022 interim final rule, Federal Register 13 October: controls on advanced computing integrated circuits, computer commodities that contain such ICs, and certain semiconductor manufacturing items. 15 January 2025 AI Diffusion interim final rule: the global diffusion of the most advanced AI models and large clusters of advanced computing ICs is written as national security and foreign policy. NVIDIA's FY26 10-K itself writes: as of the end of that fiscal year, the company was effectively foreclosed from competing in China's data center compute market. The same 10-K writes that the January diffusion rule would have put global licensing on H200, GB200 cabinets, and GB300 cabinets; in May the US government announced it would withdraw the rule and write a replacement, scope unset. That is export control being rewritten. It is not an antitrust close. DoD, November 2023, released the Data, Analytics, and AI Adoption Strategy and wired AI directly to NDS deterrence of China: competitors have widely communicated their intention to field AI for military advantage; accelerating adoption is to keep decision advantage.

These files only prove "written as national security." They do not prove "already decided not to split." That a split is hard under national security is a political constraint pushed from these files, not a signed order not to split.

## 3. Mechanism (physics, commerce, geopolitics)

### 3.1 Profit sets the bid. Buyers cannot cover

Analysis added here. Seller margins of 49% to 59.9% are already high, and those firms still expand to its mix. They are not the firms bidding against it for cabinets. On the buyer side, Microsoft has $101.8B of net income; its use is halls. Four-cloud mixed-year equipment purchases moved from $211.9B to the $354.0B order; the use is group-wide capacity, not cabinet gross margin. NVIDIA's one year of free cash $96.6B and one quarter of operating income $53.5B exist to lock the next slice of material. While the margin holds, commitments keep rising. Anyone else who wants the same material pays more, or does not get it. TSMC's board answers to shareholders. When the first name's profit exceeds the increment from everyone else, there is no financial reason to offend the first name for the second. This is not an administrative takeover. It is prepayments buying the slot. A neutrality pledge has no consideration in front of a check.

Booked 19% and packaging tightness are in [Volume 4](vol-04.en.md). This volume does not reopen them. What this volume asks is only this: buyers cannot cover, so prepayments are still the seller's.

### 3.2 Prepayments lock the next generation, not a depreciation line already disclosed

Analysis added here. The draft wrote four-year straight-line depreciation as a time bomb on the clouds' income statements. The mechanism that can be written is not an expense that already hit that line. It is the direction of the commitments. Supply commitments rose $23.8B in three months. Official text locks inventory and capacity. The prepaid manufacturing and capacity agreements sit in the 10-K. Multi-year cloud-service agreements are another line, officially for R&D, not wafer prepayments. Write them apart. Cloud-service commitments at quarter-end are on the order of $30B. That is not the same row as $119.0B of supply commitments.

The asset-light name uses profit to lock next year's slots at the heavy-asset names. NVIDIA's own capex is far smaller than the clouds' and the foundries'. The heavy assets sit in TSMC furnaces, packaging tools, and the memory makers' HBM lines. Prepayments have not already bought the whole factory dead. Some agreements can be rescheduled or cancelled. Rescheduling has a cost. Cancellation has a cost. Profit pads the cost. One year of free cash $96.6B can pad a reschedule and can pad an added tranche. Cloud money has to pay civil works, power, in-house accelerators, and cabinets at once. The same dollar cannot both build a hall and outbid at TSMC for first silicon. So the clouds are not unarmed. Their money has other uses. NVIDIA's money is used to lock the next slice of material.

The four-year versus fifteen-year mismatch still holds. The ruler has to change. Servers and cabinets are booked over four to five years. Substations, nuclear offtakes, and hall shells are booked over fifteen years or longer. The mismatch means: once this year's group-wide equipment purchases are written down, a lossless exit two years later is hard. What falls first is not a substation already poured. It is the next cabinet order and residual value on used cabinets. The draft divided $215B by four and got $45B to $55B. That treats mixed-year group-wide spend as AI hardware, then treats the assumption as an expense. This volume does not write that division as annual depreciation that already happened. What can be watched is whether next year's capex still rises, whether supply commitments still rise, and whether gross margin still returns to 75%.

### 3.3 A fine is a cost. A split changes the rules

Analysis added here. What can change the rules overnight is not AMD shipping another generation of cards. It is a split, forced interoperability, or a ban on tied supply. A fine does not change the bid. The French ceiling is about $21.6B, about four-tenths of Q1 FY27 operating income $53.5B, and about 18% of FY26 net income $120.6B. Buybacks plus dividends returned $41.1B to shareholders in a year. While there is no split, no forced-open interface, and no ban on prepayments locking capacity, a fine is a cost. US law does not punish a monopoly won on products and foresight. What it would hit is tying, supply discrimination, and using capacity to punish a stack switch. That takes contracts, mail, and customer testimony. An inquiry can run for years.

The EU sent questionnaires. Run:ai cleared unconditionally. There is no Article 101/102 opening. After a formal opening come a statement of objections, a defense, a Commission decision, then the courts. That is a process measured in years. France is near the end. The next step is whether to issue a statement of objections, or to close. Writing "near the end" as "a fine is about to land" is an add-on. The DOJ inquiry, counted from August 2024, still had no complaint as of August 2026. Subpoena reporting and the company's denial both sit in the public record. A subpoena is not a breakup order.

An 80-point accelerator share is a press and sell-side scope, not a DOJ relevant-market definition. It does not enter a finding. Seventy to eighty percent of financing flowing straight to NVIDIA has no split table. Do not write it.

### 3.4 Once the same slice of silicon is written as a license item, a split is harder

Analysis added here. The White House dislikes the fat margin drawing blood from US clouds and manufacturing, and it also needs this stack to hold the East in armaments. BIS has already written advanced computing ICs and large clusters as export-control items. DoD has already written AI adoption as part of deterrence of China. The 10-K itself writes that China's data-center compute market is effectively closed. A split or a forced-open unified compute architecture would slow its own iteration. Hypothesis from here: national security outweighs competition. What is raised high and set down is a settlement, a little give on interconnect, and in-house accelerators getting onto part of the network. The fat margin and the prepayments are not touched. Antitrust slows the walk and makes it pay protection. It does not take apart the machine that prints the profit. That is a political constraint, not an order not to split. The files do not contain the four characters "therefore do not split."

For the China stack, U.S. antitrust and export controls act on different layers. Antitrust can affect tying, interfaces, and domestic competitive conduct; it does not by itself open restricted frontier foundry or HBM capacity. China already has stronger parallel supply in smartphones and parts of consumer computing, while an equivalent frontier training stack still requires advanced logic, HBM-class memory, packaging, interconnect, and software migration to close together. The report therefore avoids the old absolute sentence "China can only do phones."

### 3.5 Where the rent flows: the application layer is squeezed; the back end collects

Analysis added here. At the front the application layer drives unit prices to the floor. At the back the rent is collected by the cabinet. NVIDIA FY26 data center $193.7B is the compute invoice the world paid for training and inference. Q4 gross margin was back to 75%. Financing and cloud bills end as cabinets. Do not write that a firm that raises $1B sends $0.7B to $0.8B into one pocket. There is no such split table. What can be written: inside four-cloud capex, training cabinets and HBM are the bulk; TrendForce puts 47% of 2026 CSP capex and 68% of 2027 into memory. That is an institutional scope. The cash flow is cloud to cabinet to NVIDIA and the three memory makers. Startups walk the cloud channel. The path is the same. The mix differs by firm.

Cloud in-house accelerators still bid for the same slice of 3nm and HBM. Public next-gen parts have not retreated to 5nm plus commodity DDR5. Miss first silicon and, to keep cloud customers, they still have to keep ordering cabinets. Hypothesis from here: the cloud becomes a channel that distributes cabinets downstream. Comprador is that meaning. It is not an already-booked order for hundreds of thousands of next-generation cabinets. Microsoft net income $101.8B. In-house accelerators have a design. They do not have their own packaging line or maker bits. Bidding against 75% gross margin is bidding against the same scarce slice.

Phones and PCs that never buy a GPU still pay the tax. TrendForce: 51% of 2026 DRAM bits are HBM plus RDIMM. Commodity LPDDR and DDR5 are residual. Price follows. Channel talk: 16G DDR5 from about 450 to 1800. Apple and Qualcomm pay a spillover on every consumer bit. That is memory contagion. It is not a check they wrote to NVIDIA.

### 3.6 Physical life and accounting life: write the mismatch; do not write an already-lost $100B a year

Analysis added here. Heat, power, and copper in a cabinet are written into fifteen-year civil works. GPU systems are booked over four to five years. If a model architecture cuts cache and memory demand before the book is run off, the prior cabinet loses its economics while depreciation is still running. A supercycle therefore has two deaths. One: incremental revenue cannot cover equipment purchases already written down. Two: revenue is enough, but the asset is obsolete before the book is run off. Both deaths hit the clouds' income statements first, not NVIDIA's gross-margin slogan. NVIDIA takes a volume hit first: clouds cut training buildout, slip the next first-to-market node, and throw inference to in-house accelerators. The price hit comes later, because the scarce slice is still in its hands.

Dividing the second forecast's depreciation by the first actuals' revenue invents a fake conclusion that a hundred billion is already being lost each year. $670B to $750B cannot be added in this pass. $121.875B is $750B times 65% divided by four: three assumptions stacked. This volume does not write that product as an expense. Power at $0.08/kWh and PUE 1.2 folding out $28B is the same class of stress-test assumption. What can be written: power is not an opex leftover. It is a capital constraint that sits next to the server. Interconnection queues are measured in years. Firms that can sign a nuclear offtake can pair fifteen-year civil works with four-year IT. Firms that cannot buy spot power and wait in line. The world's largest capex cycle is therefore not one global market. It is several regional cycles cut by power and licenses.

## 4. Analysis added (scenarios, counters, testable marks)

Analysis added here. Push forward from the numbers now on the table. Do not attach probabilities.

Main scenario: gross margin returns near 75%; supply commitments keep rising; buyer group-wide equipment purchases keep rising; the added money still cannot cover cabinet gross margin. Prepayments keep locking next-generation packaging, HBM, and first-to-market nodes. Clouds do three things: raise utilization on clusters already bought, move inference onto cheaper fabrics and in-house accelerators, and cut training buildout whose ROI cannot be told. NVIDIA takes a volume hit first, not necessarily a price hit. The application layer keeps squeezing unit prices. The back end keeps collecting by the cabinet. Phones and PCs keep paying a residual-bit tax. Inquiries stay open. Complaints stay unfiled. Whether France issues a statement of objections is still a fork, not a fine already landed. The world does not blow up. Upgrades stop one node down, and one firm writes the rules.

Bubble scenario: enterprise token volume stalls, unit prices fall, utilization weakens, and the next round of cloud CapEx or semiconductor commitments slows, is rescheduled, or is cut. What falls first can be short-lived IT residual values and new cabinet orders rather than fifteen-year civil works. A one-quarter inventory charge can move gross margin without proving that the platform's structural economics have broken. The hard-landing model therefore watches **accounting, utilization/revenue, market-clearing, and forward-commitment signals together**.

Counter A: buyers actually bundle. Prepayments large enough that TSMC cannot recognize only one buyer; software bound into one stack; memory longs that leave a line for commodity bits. All three together change allocation. Miss one and you are still picked off one by one. UXL and oneAPI are embryos. They have not punched through. Counter B: in-house accelerators take training off the cabinet, not only shunt inference. Public next-gen parts have not retreated to 5nm plus commodity memory. To beat today's cabinet you return to 3nm, HBM, and packaging, and you bid against 75% gross margin. Counter C: power stops the buildout. Interconnection and nuclear offtakes become hard constraints: cabinets have buyers, power does not. Counter D: the East stands up a second bid. Tools and materials are de-Americanized and it holds. Even then it is only a second gravity. It does not save the allocation of global cabinets. Counter E: a split or a forced-open unified compute architecture. That is the one that can change the rules overnight. The national-security files explain why that line is hard to touch. They do not show it already closed.

None of the five is done. What is done: supply commitments to $119.0B; inquiries exist, complaints do not; France is near the end and has not issued a statement of objections; the EU sent questionnaires, cleared Run:ai unconditionally, and has no Article 101/102 opening. 10% of annual turnover is a statutory ceiling. $215.9B times 10% is $21.6B. That is not a fine. White House NSM-25, BIS, and DoD write advanced chips as national security. There is no order that says "therefore do not split." Hypothesis from here: a fine is a cost. A split is hard under national security. If those five are not done, the line walks past half of the scarce slice. The window is the few years before the next first-to-market node is locked by commitments.

Testable marks: only whether public files change their wording. First, the supply-commitment row: after $95.2B to $119.0B, does the next quarter still rise or is it the first stop. Second, gross margin: Q4 75.0%, Q1 74.9%; is the next quarter still there. Third, next-year four-cloud equipment purchases: do they move from the checked set $64.5B, $128.3B, $91.4B, $69.7B, or do they jump to the unverified $200B, $190B, $150B, $130B. Fourth, does justice.gov have a *United States v. NVIDIA* complaint, does the French site have a statement of objections, does the Commission have an Article 101/102 opening release. Fifth, have the memorandum, export controls, and the defense strategy been rewritten as do-not-split or must-split. Sixth, is data-center revenue still adding on the $193.7B order. Do not treat $121.875B and $28B of opex as expenses that already happened. Do not write $21.6B as a fine about to be issued.

What the capital cycle finally has to answer is whether this round of equipment purchases will rewrite the rent in the first seven volumes. If clouds cut training buildout, push cabinet ASP down, and throw more load to open source and in-house accelerators, then 75% gross margin, the packaging lock, and HBM's share of memory revenue all flip from rent evidence to high-side risk marks. If token volume becomes a usage event, the same equipment purchases become a threshold: later names cannot match this order of annual spend, and the oligopoly is reinforced by capex itself. Both directions share one set of observations. This volume does not forecast a stock price. It does not give a multiple. It does not write "the largest of the twenty-first century" as a historical conclusion already compared. Anyone who puts $750B or $121.875B into a decision memo must copy, next to it, unverified, not an audit fact. What can be cited directly is still each firm's equipment purchases with the line item named, NVIDIA's own data-center revenue and profit cash, and the supply-commitment row.
