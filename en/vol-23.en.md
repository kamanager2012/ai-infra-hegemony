# Volume 23: HBM, memory, and “the new oil of the AI era” (HBM4 16-Hi, the logic base, and memory capex)

Chinese original: [vol-23.md](vol-23.md)

The residual model, 51% of bits, HBM and LPDDR multiplied by cabinet, and Hynix FY25 49% are [Volume 5](vol-05.md). What consumer electronics pay is a residual tax; see [Volume 22](vol-22.md). The China stack's three layers, and CXMT's 0 mentions of HBM, are [Volume 12](vol-12.md). This volume does not retell those sentences. It only writes: which stretch of 3:1 can stand on physics, whether the 16-Hi in the title has a spec sheet now, and at which point the oil metaphor breaks.

## 1. The question this volume answers

Calling HBM "new oil" — is that a metaphor or a mechanism? Oil's points are energy density, the pipe network, and rent. HBM's points are bandwidth density, stack yield, and a wafer penalty. This volume answers three things. First, how the draft's 3:1 penalty rate can stand on physics, which sentences can enter the fact layer, and which must stay draft formulas. Second, whether the title's HBM4 16-Hi and logic base die would steepen it further — and whether a spec sheet exists now. Third, at which point "new oil" fails: oil can be inventoried and delivered on the spot; HBM is bound to a given GPU generation, a given base die, and a given package.

No new cabinet counts. No new global bits. Won are not privately converted to dollars. 51% and multiply-by-cabinet are already in Volume 5. This volume only points at them when a contrast is needed.

## 2. Draft formulas (compressed; not promoted)

- TSV: die area 50% to 55% larger than same-capacity DDR5 (the Part III body uses 55%).
- 12-Hi: composite yield written as \(Y_{\text{total}}=(0.95)^{12}\times 0.90\approx 48.6\%\), input 2.5 to 3 times. The Part III flowchart also writes: one 12-inch leading-edge DRAM wafer makes about 1,200 DDR5 dice, and after area loss and yield about 400 HBM dice, still concluding 3:1. The figure also writes composite yield 54% to 65%, set beside 48.6%. Internally uncalibrated.
- Logic base die: HBM must also consume one customized leading-edge logic chip.
- HBM4: 16-Hi stack plus a TSMC 3nm / 5nm leading-edge logic base die. The penalty is written as "worsening from 3:1 further to 4:1 or even 5:1." The title's "16-Hi" lands only in that sentence. No per-stack capacity, bandwidth, production year, or price.
- GB200 NVL72: the draft writes HBM3e 13.8 TB, cost $580k to $680k, maker gross margin over 60%. The product-page official spec is 13.4 TB HBM3E, 17 TB LPDDR5X. The BOM range and "over 60%" stay draft estimates.

What can stay as the skeleton is the direction: stacking eats more silicon. What cannot stay as a meter is the 16-Hi spec, a calibrated 3:1, whole-cabinet HBM dollar cost, and "new oil that can be inventoried."

## 3. What can hold 3:1 is a maker sentence, not that 48.6%

Oil rent holds because wells, pipes, and refineries cannot be copied in one quarter. If HBM rent holds, it must land on the same class of loss that cannot be copied quickly. The draft explains 3:1 with three things: area, a yield product, and the base die. The direction of all three is right. The precision is not enough. What can enter the fact layer is not that 48.6% formula. It is the sentence Micron wrote into its own 10-K. Form 10-K for the fiscal year ended 2025-08-28, original: Due to the higher performance and more complex manufacturing process, HBM requires a higher number of wafers and more cleanroom space to produce the same number of bits as conventional DRAM in the same technology node. Source: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/723125/000072312525000028/mu-20250828.htm The same paragraph also writes: if HBM demand weakens and makers cut capacity from HBM back to conventional DRAM, conventional DRAM supply will rise materially. Read in reverse: while HBM is still taking wafers, conventional DRAM is residual. That is a sentence under maker legal liability, not a supply-chain whisper.

Hot Chips 2026 wrote the same direction in conference language. ServeTheHome floor notes: The combined overhead of design architecture, advanced packaging, and manufacturing complexity means that about 3x as much silicon is consumed to deliver the same HBM3E capacity as DDR5. Source: https://www.servethehome.com/micron-evolving-memory-architectures-for-ai-at-hot-chips-2026/ The scope must be nailed: this is same-capacity HBM3E versus DDR5, silicon consumed when design, advanced packaging, and manufacturing complexity are taken together. It is not a factory split of "one HBM wafer for three DDR5 wafers," and it is not a multiple the 10-K has already checked. 3× enters the conference-paraphrase layer.

So the economic meaning of 3:1 can stand without that uncalibrated formula: every high-bandwidth order a maker accepts gives up more conventional DRAM output. Area is eaten by TSV and KOZ. Cleanroom is eaten by stacking and the base die. Capex is eaten by sites such as Cheongju, Yongin, and Indiana. Whoever controls working TSV-stack and base-die yield has pricing power. The firms that can publicly volume-produce high-bandwidth stacks are still Hynix, Micron, and Samsung. CXMT's prospectus has 0 mentions of HBM. A fourth name cannot be written into 2026 effective supply.

Bit share still understates how much wafer is pulled away. The 51% nailed in Volume 5 is already HBM plus RDIMM bits. HBM's own bit share must be smaller than 51%. Micron's 10-K and the conference 3× both say: for the same bits, HBM eats more wafers and cleanroom. A direct inference, no new percentage invented: HBM's weight in wafers and cleanroom is higher than its weight in bits. One maker sentence on conversion, and consumption loses more than 1:1. All that can be said is direction — move a line to stacking, and residual falls by more than the bits moved. That is why "shift onto DDR5" looks like saving HBM on the books and like a tax on the wafer.

## 4. 16-Hi has no spec sheet; if the base die enters TSMC, a slice of rent moves

If the base die runs 3nm / 5nm, HBM is no longer "the memory maker's own affair." It has to queue at TSMC. The draft writes the HBM4 16-Hi penalty as 4:1 to 5:1, because layers walk from 12 to 16, the yield product gets steeper, and one leading-edge logic chip is added. Direction can stay. The meter cannot. Hynix official text writes HBM3E and HBM4 supplied at the same time, and HBM4 already in large-scale production. No 16-Hi. No yield formula. No 3:1. Samsung official text writes HBM4 delivery starting Q1 2026, including 11.7Gbps. Also no 16-Hi. Micron's 10-K writes HBM4 36GB 12-Hi already sampled. The stack height that can be publicly nailed is 12-Hi, not the title's 16.

So a stretch of the "new oil" pipe network may be laid in someone else's fab. Until makers and TSMC both give checkable base-die shipments, that migration is mechanism, not a profit reallocation already done. Official TSMC sentences this pass can still use: advanced packaging and test revenue about 8% in 2024, a bit over 10% in 2025; C.C. Wei, CoWoS tight / cannot meet demand; 2Q26, limits customers' growth. There is no split that says "how much 3nm the HBM base die ate."

The title puts 16-Hi beside the logic base die exactly to flag this migration. If the base die stays on the memory maker's mature process, rent stays mostly with Korean makers. If the base die must enter TSMC, a slice of rent moves to foundry, and HBM is no longer a single industry's well. The watch item can stay. The fact cannot be written.

## 5. The oil metaphor breaks in three places

HBM can almost never be used as inventory oil. Oil in a tank can still wait for the price. HBM's interface, stack height, and base die are bound to a given GPU generation. Four-year hardware depreciation means one generation of dice can hardly be sold at the original price across two system generations. Crude has a public benchmark and a futures curve. HBM is an annual or semi-annual large-customer negotiation. The price almost never appears in a 10-K line this report can cite. "New oil" therefore slides into narrative more easily: in shortage everyone says it is like oil; in glut there is no future in which to short a generation of 12-Hi dice. If the investment side holds memory stocks as oil companies, it must accept no inventory buffer, no public curve, only generation lock. The enterprise side should treat HBM as the threshold part without which a cabinet cannot ship, not as a consumable that can be topped up on the spot market.

MLA compressing KV Cache by 93.3% (draft number) is demand-side substitute energy. It does not make HBM vanish. It lowers "resident video memory per token." If firms turn the saving at once into longer context and higher concurrency, demand is still there. If they cannot, capex sunk into higher stacks and a leading-edge base die becomes an idle well. NVL72 writing 13.4 TB as unified memory is half defined by the system: NVLink first welds 72 cards into one logical chip, and only then does HBM look like fuel that must be bought by the cabinet. If more workloads can later be split inside the memory wall, the unit of measure walks from "whole-cabinet TB" back to "per-card GB." Until that day, procurement racing for 8-Hi / 12-Hi by the cabinet looks a lot like racing for crude berths at the dock. This volume does not forecast the year of that day. Once the unit of measure changes, the words "new oil" have to be rewritten, because oil rarely stops needing tanks just because the combustion algorithm improved.

Custom stacks tear the oil metaphor one more slit. Hynix's FY25 press release writes Custom HBM as a differentiator that continues to draw attention. This is not a volume number. It is a product form. Custom means stack, base die, and channel count are cut to one accelerator. Still less can it be flipped as inventory crude on a spot board. The coach sets the mix. The maker changes the tool to the mix. The die binds tighter to the generation, the cabinet, even a single customer. If the investment side still holds on "the memory cycle is warming," what it has misread is not profit elasticity. It is the inventory assumption.

On geography, three Korean-US-Japanese makers plus a possible TSMC base die make HBM easier to cut with export controls than a logic GPU. Reading HBM as a national "oil reserve" overstates tradability. It cannot be flexibly delivered on a spot market the way crude can. The reserve logic should be written as "working stack capacity plus foundry access for the base die," not tonnage.

## 6. Three scenarios, and what not to do

Three scenarios. No new probabilities attached:

- **New oil holds.** 12-Hi walks steady on the official already-shipping scope. The penalty still earns its keep economically. Hynix operating margin still near 49%; Micron CMBU still far thicker than almost-flat MCBU. If 16-Hi still appears only on a roadmap, that does not deny this scenario. What holds it is half the bits and maker books, not the stack height in the title.
- **Base-die bottleneck.** Memory makers announce the next generation. Shipments are stuck on a leading-edge logic base die or a packaging quota. What it looks like: maker press releases keep writing HBM4; what whole cabinets lack is "a complete solderable HBM set," not DRAM wafers themselves. A slice of rent moves to foundry.
- **Algorithms or architecture cut demand.** A jump like MLA / MoE spreads, or clouds actually adopt what TrendForce itself wrote — "reduce the HBM integrated into each AI chip." Maker margins fall back from 49%. The 10-K sentence "same bits need more wafers" still holds on physics, but no longer earns its keep economically. Conventional DRAM flows back on Micron's 10-K reverse sentence.

The other side must be written fully, or "new oil" inflates on its own. First: about 1,200 dice versus about 400, and 48.6% set beside the figure's 54% to 65%, show that draft 3:1 is an order-of-magnitude conclusion, not a calibrated process-control chart. Second: Hynix 49% and Micron CMBU $13.52B contain a present shortage premium. They are not long-run equilibrium rent. Third: calling HBM oil makes people think demand has no elasticity. Fourth: 51% is bit supply, not revenue. 47% / 68% are not four-cloud 10-K line items.

Testable: whether Micron's next 10-K still writes "same bits need more wafers"; whether HBM4 is 12-Hi samples or a higher stack shipping; whether Hynix and Samsung official pages show a 16-Hi spec sheet; whether TSMC earnings calls have a checkable base-die sentence.

What not to do: do not write 16-Hi as already in volume production; do not write 3:1 as a factory control chart; do not multiply 13.4 TB out to 50,000 cabinets and then divide by unpublished global HBM bits; do not convert Hynix and Samsung won into dollars and add them to Micron; do not write the CXMT IPO into the HBM supply function. The full residual and multiply-by-cabinet write-up is [Volume 5](vol-05.md).
