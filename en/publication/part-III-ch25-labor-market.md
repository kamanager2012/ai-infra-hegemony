# Part III · Chapter 25: AI and Labor Markets — The Task-to-Job Translation Layer

> **Role in the report — second-order transmission module.** Labor-market evidence is used to examine downstream consequences of workflow adoption. It does not validate semiconductor scarcity, platform concentration, or capital clearing, and it does not independently move the S1–S4 baseline.

## 1. AI changes tasks before job structures

Jobs are bundles of tasks, and those tasks differ in automation cost, review burden, liability, physical presence, and trust.

The analytical starting point is task and workflow exposure—not direct job-loss extrapolation.

## 2. Exposure is not an employment outcome

ILO and IMF research both require a separation between occupational exposure and actual adoption, job redesign, and employment outcomes.

ILO's 2025 refined index estimates that roughly **one in four jobs worldwide has some GenAI exposure**, while emphasizing that transformation is more likely than simple redundancy.[^1]

The IMF framework estimates around **40% of global employment** and about **60% of advanced-economy employment** as exposed to AI.[^2]

These are occupational / employment exposure measures.

They are not "the same percentage of tasks will be automated," and they are not equivalent to the same percentage of jobs disappearing.

## 2A. 2026 shows localized signals, not a general labor-market verdict

Stanford AI Index 2026 reports that U.S. employment for **software developers aged 22–25 fell by nearly 20% from 2024**, while roughly one-third of surveyed organizations expect AI to reduce workforce over the following year. At the same time, broad employment data have not shown job losses on the same scale across the economy.[^3]

This evidence has to be read narrowly:

- it refers to a specific country, age cohort, and occupation;
- hiring cycles, macro conditions, graduate supply, and organizational restructuring can also affect the observed change;
- it does not establish that AI alone caused a 20% decline.

Anthropic's June 2026 Economic Index provides a different early signal: respondents who use Claude in more automated ways tend to anticipate that AI will be able to perform a larger share of their tasks over the next 12 months.[^4] Anthropic explicitly notes that the survey is not representative of the general population and over-represents computer/mathematical and management occupations.

The defensible 2026 judgment remains:

> **Localized hiring-pipeline and early-career signals are visible, but the translation from task exposure to workflow adoption to employment outcome is still unfolding.**

## 3. Task-to-Job Translation requires multiple gates

The labor-market transmission chain is:

> **Model Capability → Task Suitability → Workflow Redesign → Adoption Economics / Responsibility → Job Composition → Employment / Wage Outcome**

The importance of this chain is that any layer can interrupt a direct extrapolation from better model capability to fewer jobs.

For example:

- a model being able to perform a task does not mean the firm's workflow has been redesigned;
- a workflow being automatable does not mean error, compliance, review, and responsibility costs are low enough;
- compression of one task does not imply disappearance of the whole occupation;
- higher output per employee can show up as demand expansion rather than headcount reduction.

Employment analysis should begin with tasks and workflows instead of jumping directly from benchmark performance to job counts.

## 4. Adoption economics is the first substitution gate

Firms have stronger substitution incentives only when:

> **AI cost + review + error + compliance + integration < human cost / value alternative**

Benchmark improvement does not automatically translate into employment change.

Reliability, latency, security, integration, and legal responsibility all alter adoption economics.

## 5. Exposure is highest where tasks are digital, repeatable, and evaluable

Examples include:

- drafting;
- summarization;
- classification;
- routine coding;
- standard support;
- research assistance.

These tasks have digital inputs/outputs and relatively clear evaluation criteria, making them easier to compress through agent/workflow systems.

Task compression can still appear as higher output per worker rather than immediate elimination of the job.

## 6. Responsibility constraints preserve human review

Medical sign-off, engineering sign-off, field maintenance, nuclear operations, complex negotiation, and care work are constrained by liability, physical presence, licensing, and trust.

AI can change preparation, analysis, and documentation while final responsibility remains with a human.

Human review is both a software cost and a labor-market boundary.

## 7. The entry-level ladder is a key structural variable

Even if total employment does not fall sharply in the short run, traditional entry-level tasks in junior analysis, junior coding, entry-level support, and routine research can be compressed first.

A conventional career ladder often looks like:

> junior work → experience accumulation → senior judgment

If junior tasks are heavily automated, firms and education systems have to redesign how new workers accumulate experience.

This structural effect can matter more than a single economy-wide unemployment forecast.

## 8. Wage outcomes can diverge

AI can increase leverage for high-skill workers while lowering the value of routine tasks and raising the premium on system, operator, and verification skills.

Possible outcomes include:

- higher productivity for top performers;
- contraction in middle routine roles;
- stronger demand for physical / system technical roles.

Labor outcomes are more likely to diverge structurally than to produce the same wage decline across all knowledge workers.

## 9. Physical AI can create a slower second wave

Automation in warehouses, manufacturing, logistics, inspection, and maintenance has to cross hardware cost, reliability, safety, maintenance, and regulatory thresholds.

The labor translation of Physical AI is usually slower and more capital-intensive than purely digital workflow automation.

## 10. AI infrastructure creates new skill demand

AI expansion simultaneously increases demand for:

- data-center technicians;
- liquid-cooling engineers;
- power engineers;
- semiconductor process engineers;
- advanced-packaging engineers;
- network engineers;
- safety/compliance roles;
- model-infrastructure roles.

Labor-market change is better understood as reallocation than as a one-direction reduction in employment.

## 11. Organization shape depends on review burden and task volume

Agent-native workflows can reduce some coordination work.

But if task volume, exception handling, review, and compliance rise simultaneously, headcount does not necessarily fall in the same proportion.

More useful observables include:

- management layers;
- span of control;
- junior/senior ratio;
- revenue per employee;
- human-review ratio.

## 12. Current read

As of 2026, there is enough evidence to conclude that AI is restructuring tasks.

There is not enough cross-country evidence to support one universal "economy-wide job disappearance rate."

The correct sequence remains:

> **first observe task automation → then workflow redesign → finally employment / wage outcomes**

Any framework that converts an exposure percentage directly into an unemployment forecast skips the most important corporate-adoption and responsibility layers.
