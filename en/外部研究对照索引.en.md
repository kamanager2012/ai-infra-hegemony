# External Research Comparison Index

> This page is not an "authority endorsement list." Its purpose is to compare this report with publicly searchable institutional research, first-party disclosures, and industry datasets, clarifying which conclusions are externally covered and which are the report's own cross-layer mechanisms and scenario synthesis.  
> **Positioning rule:** comparisons are dimension-by-dimension. This report does not claim global uniqueness, universal comprehensiveness, or superiority to specialized systems such as SemiAnalysis, IEA, Epoch AI, sell-side research, or academic datasets. Its differentiator is the combination of cross-layer synthesis, denominator discipline, claim identity, and falsifiable scenario adjudication.  
> **Retrieval and revision cutoff: August 28, 2026**

## 1. OECD — AI Infrastructure Competition

**Report:** *Competition in Artificial Intelligence Infrastructure*  
**Institution:** OECD  
**Date:** 2025  
**URL:** https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/competition-in-artificial-intelligence-infrastructure_623d1874-en.html

OECD focuses on:

- AI accelerators and compute access;
- infrastructure supply chains;
- concentration and entry barriers;
- vertical integration;
- strategic partnerships;
- competition policy.

Relationship to this report:

- **Shared ground:** AI is not a pure-software market; physical infrastructure, interface control, and vertical integration can shape competition.
- **This report adds:** a deeper link from competition policy into advanced logic, HBM, advanced packaging, LPS/power, quantity assurance, Anchor Buyers, and Financing Reflexivity.
- **Boundary:** OECD's analysis of competition risks is not a legal monopoly finding against any particular company.

## 2. IEA — Energy and AI

**Institution:** International Energy Agency  
**Date:** 2025–2026  
**URLs:**

- https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai
- https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai

IEA provides:

- data-center electricity baselines and scenarios;
- grid, generation, and permitting constraints;
- efficiency and demand scenarios;
- connection and infrastructure-development clocks.

Relationship to this report:

- **Shared ground:** power is an independent physical constraint on AI infrastructure.
- **This report adds:** a state model separating **Contracted / Secured / Permitted / Construction / Energized / Utilized**, while keeping generation GW, PPA capacity, and IT-GW separate.
- **Boundary:** a global electricity scenario, interconnection queue, or project announcement is not evidence of usable AI load at a specific site.

## 4. SIA + Deloitte — The AI Rack Semiconductor Stack

**Report:** *Powering AI: The Semiconductor Ecosystem at the Foundation of Data Centers*  
**Institution:** Semiconductor Industry Association + Deloitte  
**Date:** 2026  
**Entry point:** https://www.semiconductors.org/new-report-finds-semiconductors-account-for-95-of-an-ai-data-server-racks-value-encompassing-the-full-stack-of-chip-technologies/

The study emphasizes that an AI rack is not "one GPU." It is a semiconductor system spanning compute, memory, networking, control, and related components.

Relationship to this report:

- **Shared ground:** the unit of competition and sale is moving from chip toward system / rack.
- **This report adds:** the connection from rack architecture into advanced logic, HBM, packaging, power, LPS, financing, and workload utilization.
- **Boundary:** rack BOM structure cannot directly infer global shipments, HBM consumption, or supplier allocation.

## 5. Micron — HBM Manufacturing-Input Trade Ratio

**Source:** Micron FY24 Q2 Prepared Remarks  
**URL:** https://s25.q4cdn.com/621799436/files/doc_financials/2024/q2/FY24-Q2-Prepared-Remarks.pdf

Core first-party fact:

> Under **same technology node / same number of bits** conditions, HBM3E requires approximately **3× the wafer supply of DDR5**.

Relationship to this report:

- **Shared ground:** HBM expansion changes manufacturing input, not only selling price.
- **This report adds:** the connection from the trade ratio into Non-AI Residual Capacity, the Two-Speed Semiconductor Economy, and memory product-mix rebalancing.
- **Boundary:** 3× is not a price multiple, not a permanent constant for every generation, and not a direct HBM wafer-share estimate.

## 6. TrendForce — HBM, CoWoS, Allocation, and Consumer Transmission

The report uses TrendForce mainly as **B-grade institutional research**, not as company first-party disclosure.

Key observations include:

- 2026E HBM + RDIMM at roughly 51% of DRAM bit supply;
- NVIDIA estimated above 60% of 2025 CoWoS demand;
- memory allocation fulfillment and quantity assurance;
- 2026 smartphone / PC memory-price pressure;
- moderation in mobile-DRAM price growth later in 2026.

Relationship to this report:

- **Shared ground:** AI/server demand is changing memory and packaging product mix and allocation.
- **This report adds:** the integration of these signals into **AI Resource Gravity, Non-AI Residual Capacity, Quantity Assurance, and Rolling Bottleneck Cascade**.
- **Counterevidence value:** moderating mobile-DRAM price growth shows that resource pressure is not mechanically one-way; inventory, demand elasticity, and supply response still matter.
- **Boundary:** TrendForce demand estimates cannot be upgraded into TSMC or memory-vendor official customer allocation.

- 2026 capital budget: US$60B–64B;
- advanced-packaging tightness still limits customer growth;

## 11. China's Parallel Stack — External Data Must Be Read Layer by Layer

The report primarily uses:

- CXMT / Shanghai Stock Exchange;
- YMTC official materials;
- Counterpoint;
- TrendForce foundry data;
- ASML export-control materials;
- DeepSeek technical reports;
- company product pages.

Current external evidence supports:

- materially stronger domestic capability in consumer/conventional memory and selected compute;
- production-grade substitution that can no longer be described as "no domestic system exists";
- the need to keep conventional DRAM / NAND, smartphone SoC, and foundry revenue share separate from autonomous frontier closure.

The report therefore uses two levels:

1. **Production-Grade Parallel Stack**
2. **Autonomous Frontier Closure**

The second level additionally requires equipment / materials / software / service continuity and cross-generation infrastructure scaling.

## 12. ILO — Task Exposure Is Not Job Loss

**Report:** *Generative AI and Jobs: A Refined Global Index of Occupational Exposure*  
**Institution:** International Labour Organization  
**Date:** 2025

External anchors:

- roughly one quarter of global jobs have some GenAI exposure;
- the highest exposure category is around 3.3%;
- most jobs are more likely to be transformed than directly eliminated.

The report uses:

```text
Model Capability
→ Task Suitability
→ Workflow Redesign
→ Adoption Economics / Responsibility
→ Job Composition
→ Employment / Wage Outcome
```

No exposure percentage should be converted directly into an unemployment forecast.

## 13. Rules for Using External Research

External research can support:

- historical / current facts;
- institution-owned forecasts;
- industry definitions;
- competition and technical structure;
- counterevidence;
- scenario calibration.

It cannot be upgraded directly as follows:

- institutional forecast → company actual;
- demand share → allocation share;
- revenue share → wafer share;
- secured capacity → energized load;
- high concentration → legal monopoly;
- model capability → job loss;
- production-grade substitution → autonomous frontier closure.

## 14. The Report's Cross-Layer Research Question

Public research can separately cover GPUs, HBM, advanced packaging, data centers, power, CapEx, and labor.

This report focuses on the transmission among them:

> **AI high-value demand → layer-specific scarce-capacity access → system coordination → capital formation / financing → Non-AI Residual Capacity → industry transmission → utilization / monetization → next-cycle capital allocation**

The variables that require continuous validation are:

- which scarce layers are tightening or loosening;
- who receives quantity assurance;
- whether system substitution creates production-scale economics;
- whether financing increasingly depends on sponsor / seller support;
- whether traditional markets regain residual supply;
- which reverse signals are strong enough to change S1–S4 weighting.
