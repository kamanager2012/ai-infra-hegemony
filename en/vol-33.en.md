# Volume 33: AMD, Intel, Qualcomm, and Apple — Differentiated Competitive Paths

Chinese original: [vol-33.md](vol-33.en.md)

Scarce-layer value capture, supply commitments, and the current financial base: see [Volume 1](vol-01.en.md) and the master verdict. This volume does not recopy that table. It only writes which slice of bid and slots each of the four is stuck on, and why they are not an alliance.

## 1. Core question

AMD, Intel, Qualcomm, and Apple are not a unified counter-alliance to NVIDIA. They occupy different positions in the system stack and pursue different strategic paths:

- **AMD:** direct accelerator and system competition;
- **Intel:** x86, accelerator, and foundry/industrial-policy paths;
- **Qualcomm:** on-device AI and power-efficient edge computing;
- **Apple:** vertically integrated device computing and local inference.

The relevant question is not whether “the traditional four” win or lose together, but which layers each can control, which scarce resources they still need to buy, and where their strategies complement or conflict with hyperscaler system stacks.
## 2. The checked base: strong seller-side cash flow and capacity-reservation power

Profit, cash, and supply commitments: see [Volume 1](vol-01.en.md). This volume does not recopy the table. Among the four traditional majors, none has published a prepaid manufacturing and capacity total of the same order. That is not a brand gap. It is a bid gap.

## 3. Where each of the four is stuck is not one counter-path

All four look as if they are making AI chips. They are bidding for the same scarce set: TSMC leading-edge process, advanced packaging, and consumer memory not yet drained by HBM. Every extra slice of packaging AMD takes is a slice Apple's M series and Broadcom ASICs do not get. Qualcomm does not build training cabinets, yet it still has to buy the same-generation wafers. So "the four jointly counter" does not hold commercially. earlier stress-test material has no alliance, no cross-license, and no joint prepayment.

### 3.1 AMD: closest to the training-cabinet door, still a buyer

AMD has Instinct accelerators. It is the only one of the four still standing at the training-cluster door. The short board is not "can it design a GPU." It is two walls: bid and software. Resource access remains a constraint. NVIDIA's supply-related commitments reached $119.0B at FY27 Q1, but customer-level allocation is undisclosed. AMD therefore competes on product, software, system integration, order scale, and supplier access rather than on GPU design alone. Software: CUDA, NVLink, and the cabinet standard are software/interface control. ROCm / oneAPI are competitors. In ten years they have not taken the right to define the training cabinet. No official sample was found for "95% of the world's frontier operators are bound to CUDA" or "AMD inference actually lags 30%–50%." Those two sentences do not enter the fact layer.

What it is more likely to eat first is a price-sensitive inference cluster that can live with Ethernet and a relatively regular model shape, not a training cabinet that treats 72 cards as one chip. That is a position call, not a shipment forecast. Instinct revenue and a CoWoS-by-customer quota have no official split in the current evidence review. Leave them empty.

### 3.2 Intel: foundry and accelerators are two clocks. Neither has won

Intel is fighting the wafer fab and the accelerator at once. The foundry path is won or lost on leading-edge yield and customer trust, not on a brand speech. The accelerator path is won or lost on whether the compiler stays stable on dynamic shapes and long context. the current evidence review did not read a latest full Intel 10-K accelerator-compute split. Do not fill the blank with "about to reverse," and do not write "already wiped out." x86 attach, host, and PC CPUs are still there. There is no official allocation table proving who cannot get capacity.

Intel also has a national industrial-policy path: treating the fab itself as a sovereign asset. CHIPS Act grants, fab production dates, yields: earlier stress-test material is empty. This volume does not invent them. A sovereign can wait ten years to climb the ladder. Terminals that cannot wait cannot wait for that ladder.

### 3.3 Qualcomm: the flagship can wait a while; the downstream cannot

Qualcomm's field is the flagship phone and the on-device NPU in PCs. It does not sell an NVL72-class cabinet. What it pads is not 71%–75% cabinet gross margin. What it wants is an SoC premium. When leading-edge process and HBM feed the data center first, consumer memory and phone logic get squeezed. That is the resource-allocation pressure, not Qualcomm helping AMD bid for cabinets.

earlier stress-test material wrote flagship SoC purchase prices and a replacement cycle. the current evidence review did not verify them. Do not promote them. The mechanism that can be written: an on-device breakout is not another bin of peak TOPS. It is splitting a local small model and a cloud large model under a given BOM constraint. If price rises and experience does not, inflation passes to the OEM, then to the user, demand shrinks, and leading-edge orders concentrate further on the data center and Apple. Mid- and low-end phones, channel boxes, and security modules have no prepayments. The queue does not reach them. The first wave squeezes those, not Qualcomm the company.

### 3.4 Apple: a vertical closed loop. It can wait. It does not fight the training cabinet

Apple keeps chip, system, device, and distribution in one house. It does not need to win cloud training. It only needs the on-device model to be enough on its own unified memory, and to keep everyday intelligence on the device. Jensen Huang says NVIDIA is already TSMC's largest customer. The 20-F does not name names. TSMC 2025 first customer 19% (up from 12%), second customer 17% (down from 22%). Booked 19% is not a safe distance. Occupy the increment and next year's slots belong to the leading buyer. Apple is a major leading-edge buyer for device computing, while NVIDIA is a major HPC buyer. Their demand can overlap at the foundry level even though their end markets and system strategies differ.

Apple can wait, because the device loop and the cash flow do not turn over on a quarterly channel shelf. It cannot change the cabinet's system premium, and it does not sell cloud training outward. Writing Apple as one member of "the traditional four counter-attack" mislabels a buyer who can wait as a challenger.

## 6. Hyperscaler system stacks are a separate competitive force

Specs: see [Volume 4](vol-04.en.md). The Samsung long is Tesla: see [Volume 25](vol-25.en.md). TPU, Trainium, Maia, and MTIA are not in this volume's title, yet they will eat the internal-inference slice AMD is most likely to take. The products exist. the current evidence review has no official training-share split. They have not yet displaced the dominant external platform at comparable scale. The report does not treat "the counter-attack is about to succeed." The report does not treat "they can only be wiped out."

## 7. Cut system OEM from consumer notebooks. Sony is not Microsoft

On the AI-cabinet slice, OEMs shipping DGX-class / certified systems is already happening. Consumer notebooks becoming "assembled to NVIDIA drawings" is not done, and there is no shipment proof. Dell, Lenovo, ASUS, MSI, and HP becoming system ODMs is an extension of the cabinet logic that has already happened, not a fact already delivered on consumer notebooks. The wafer foundry is still TSMC. PC brands do not become fabs.

Sony thinning into a software game brand is plausible. Microsoft will not. Microsoft's cloud slice: see [Volume 8](vol-08.en.md). Xbox can thin. Azure will not. None of the four is a cloud. Writing their end state as "all become losers under NVIDIA" is too full. The rivals are still here today. There is no official "already wiped out" data on CPUs, phone SoCs, or x86 attach. The report does not treat who exits in which year.

Game-card supply yields: see [Volume 2](vol-02.en.md) and [Volume 32](vol-32.en.md). That is not about the four counter-attacking. It does decide that their downstream is squeezed first. The first wave squeezes PCs, commercial PCs, general-purpose servers, phones, plus security and the channel. It does not squeeze Apple first.

## 9. Who can wait, who cannot

The clock that can wait is years and a decade. Intelligent Cloud is still growing. NVIDIA data center FY26 $193,737M. Foundry and memory demand are pulled by the system roadmap and workload mix. States decide on licenses and safety stock. The clock that cannot wait is quarterly shipments and shelf turns. When leading-edge process, packaging, and HBM feed a 71%–75% data-center margin first, consumer cards are already official "limited supply." On the same kit, those who cannot wait are squeezed first.

Among the four: Apple can wait; Qualcomm's flagship can wait a while; AMD can wait at the inference-cabinet door and still has not taken the right to define the training cabinet; Intel's foundry clock is longer, and as long as accelerators are still in the filing they have not been wiped out. Downstream mid- and low-end phones, channel PCs, and security modules have no $119.0B-class supply commitment. The queue does not reach them. the current evidence review has no security or channel shipment stats. The mechanism can be written. The report does not infer a name list.

## 10. Testable markers and gaps

Testable, leave empty if there is no number: whether Intel / AMD accelerator-compute revenue is still in the filings; whether the next-generation console APU is still AMD; whether consumer notebooks can still ship a non-NVIDIA CPU design in volume; whether OEM AI servers are down to a certified list only; whether TSMC's first-customer share keeps moving off 19%; whether supply commitments keep rising; whether justice.gov shows *United States v. NVIDIA*; whether France issues a statement of objections; whether the EU shows an Article 101/102 opening.

What cannot be treated as fact: a joint counter-attack by the four; PTX 95%; AMD lagging 30%–50%; a CoWoS-by-customer 14%/8%; flagship SoC purchase prices; a 40-month replacement cycle; latest full gross margins for the four (not opened file by file in the current evidence review); prepayment dollars split to TSMC or 2nm; DOJ already having sued; a 10%-of-annual-turnover fine about to be issued; the White House already having written a no-split order. A backdoor is an allegation. It does not enter the fact layer. A privileged position (training has to pass the stack and the cabinet) is structure.

This volume does not write the four as a counter-alliance about to succeed, and it does not write them as only able to be wiped out. Each is still there. Bid and slots sit with the leading buyer. Regulation is still at inquiry. National-security files explain a political constraint, not a no-split order.

## 15. How residual-capacity pressure reaches their downstream markets

Game-card supply yields: see [Volume 2](vol-02.en.md). The first wave does not squeeze Apple. It squeezes PCs, commercial PCs, general-purpose servers, phones, plus security and the channel. Security, the channel, and white-box have no four-cloud mixed-year spend, and even less a supply-commitment prepayment. The queue does not reach them. the current evidence review has no security or channel shipment stats. Do not name who dies.

Servers have to be split: AI cabinets are swelling. There is no "shipments already wiped out" series for general-purpose servers. CPU down-binning can be written. Wipe-out cannot. Grace shows they also do not want to leave the CPU to a rival. Console APUs, phone SoCs, and x86 attach are all still there. Dells shipping certified systems is a cabinet slice that has already happened. Consumer notebooks assembled to drawings is not done. The wafer foundry is still TSMC.

The four sit in different places on this resource-allocation pressure. Apple's device loop can keep everyday intelligence on-device and can bear wafer price-up for a stretch. Qualcomm writing the price-up into the SoC is using phone profit to yield wafers to the cloud. AMD wants a cloud substitute, yet it bids against Apple and Broadcom for the same packaging slice. Intel wants foundry sovereignty. That clock is longer than a consumer shelf, and slower than the right to define the training cabinet. A joint counter-attack does not hold, because they bid against one another for material.
