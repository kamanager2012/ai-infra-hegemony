# Volume 33: AMD, Intel, Qualcomm, Apple (counter-paths of the four legacy compute giants and on-device AI)

Chinese original: [vol-33.md](vol-33.md)

A margin monopoly, supply commitments, and the FY26 base: see [Volume 1](vol-01.md) and the master verdict. This volume does not recopy that table. It only writes which slice of bid and slots each of the four is stuck on, and why they are not an alliance.

## 1. The question this volume answers

Are the four traditional compute majors in the title still there? Yes. Have they formed an alliance to counter NVIDIA? No. The draft has no alliance agreement either. This volume changes the question: under a margin monopoly, not a share monopoly, is each of the four a buyer or a seller, who can wait and who cannot, and can an antitrust inquiry give them a shortcut.

Who can wait: clouds, NVIDIA, the memory makers, sovereigns. Who cannot: PCs, mid- and low-end phones, security cameras, the channel. Among the four, Apple sits closer to those who can wait. Qualcomm's flagship can wait a while. White-box and security are not in this volume's title, yet they are the first to lose the line. Writing "the four compute majors" as siphon victims is the wrong layer.

## 2. The checked base: a margin monopoly, prepayments still rising

Profit, cash, and supply commitments: see [Volume 1](vol-01.md). This volume does not recopy the table. Among the four traditional majors, none has published a prepaid manufacturing and capacity total of the same order. That is not a brand gap. It is a bid gap.

## 3. Where each of the four is stuck is not one counter-path

All four look as if they are making AI chips. They are bidding for the same scarce set: TSMC leading-edge process, advanced packaging, and consumer memory not yet drained by HBM. Every extra slice of packaging AMD takes is a slice Apple's M series and Broadcom ASICs do not get. Qualcomm does not build training cabinets, yet it still has to buy the same-generation wafers. So "the four jointly counter" does not hold commercially. The draft has no alliance, no cross-license, and no joint prepayment.

### 3.1 AMD: closest to the training-cabinet door, still a buyer

AMD has Instinct accelerators. It is the only one of the four still standing at the training-cluster door. The short board is not "can it design a GPU." It is two walls: bid and software. Bid: supply commitments are already at $119.0B. Official text does not split customers, but the mechanism is: when the first name's profit exceeds the increment from everyone else, the foundry has no financial reason to give slots to the second. Software: CUDA, NVLink, and the cabinet standard are referee rights. ROCm / oneAPI are competitors. In ten years they have not taken the right to define the training cabinet. No official sample was found for "95% of the world's frontier operators are bound to CUDA" or "AMD inference actually lags 30%–50%." Those two sentences do not enter the fact layer.

What it is more likely to eat first is a price-sensitive inference cluster that can live with Ethernet and a relatively regular model shape, not a training cabinet that treats 72 cards as one chip. That is a position call, not a shipment forecast. Instinct revenue and a CoWoS-by-customer quota have no official split in this pass. Leave them empty.

### 3.2 Intel: foundry and accelerators are two clocks. Neither has won

Intel is fighting the wafer fab and the accelerator at once. The foundry path is won or lost on leading-edge yield and customer trust, not on a brand speech. The accelerator path is won or lost on whether the compiler stays stable on dynamic shapes and long context. This pass did not read a latest full Intel 10-K accelerator-compute split. Do not fill the blank with "about to reverse," and do not write "already wiped out." x86 attach, host, and PC CPUs are still there. There is no official allocation table proving who cannot get capacity.

Intel also has a national industrial-policy path: treating the fab itself as a sovereign asset. CHIPS Act grants, fab production dates, yields: the draft is empty. This volume does not invent them. A sovereign can wait ten years to climb the ladder. Terminals that cannot wait cannot wait for that ladder.

### 3.3 Qualcomm: the flagship can wait a while; the downstream cannot

Qualcomm's field is the flagship phone and the on-device NPU in PCs. It does not sell an NVL72-class cabinet. What it pads is not 71%–75% cabinet gross margin. What it wants is an SoC premium. When leading-edge process and HBM feed the data center first, consumer memory and phone logic get squeezed. That is the siphon, not Qualcomm helping AMD bid for cabinets.

The draft wrote flagship SoC purchase prices and a replacement cycle. This pass did not verify them. Do not promote them. The mechanism that can be written: an on-device breakout is not another bin of peak TOPS. It is splitting a local small model and a cloud large model under a given BOM constraint. If price rises and experience does not, inflation passes to the OEM, then to the user, demand shrinks, and leading-edge orders concentrate further on the data center and Apple. Mid- and low-end phones, channel boxes, and security modules have no prepayments. The queue does not reach them. The first wave squeezes those, not Qualcomm the company.

### 3.4 Apple: a vertical closed loop. It can wait. It does not fight the training cabinet

Apple keeps chip, system, device, and distribution in one house. It does not need to win cloud training. It only needs the on-device model to be enough on its own unified memory, and to keep everyday intelligence on the device. Jensen Huang says NVIDIA is already TSMC's largest customer. The 20-F does not name names. TSMC 2025 first customer 19% (up from 12%), second customer 17% (down from 22%). Booked 19% is not a safe distance. Occupy the increment and next year's slots belong to the first name. Apple locks phone logic. NVIDIA locks the HPC increment. They bid for the same fab. They are not an alliance.

Apple can wait, because the device loop and the cash flow do not turn over on a quarterly channel shelf. It cannot change the cabinet's system premium, and it does not sell cloud training outward. Writing Apple as one member of "the traditional four counter-attack" mislabels a buyer who can wait as a challenger.

## 4. Antitrust is not a shortcut

The docket: see [Volume 11](vol-11.md) and [Volume 31](vol-31.md). If the four are counting on regulators to hand them the right to define the training cabinet, the public record does not match. Inquiries exist. Complaints do not. None of the four has received an official packaging quota or a CUDA interface because of an inquiry. Writing "regulators are about to open the market" as the four's counter-attack timetable exceeds the public record.

## 7. Cut system OEM from consumer notebooks. Sony is not Microsoft

On the AI-cabinet slice, OEMs shipping DGX-class / certified systems is already happening. Consumer notebooks becoming "assembled to NVIDIA drawings" is not done, and there is no shipment proof. Dell, Lenovo, ASUS, MSI, and HP becoming system ODMs is an extension of the cabinet logic that has already happened, not a fact already delivered on consumer notebooks. The wafer foundry is still TSMC. PC brands do not become fabs.

Sony thinning into a software game brand is plausible. Microsoft will not. Microsoft's cloud slice: see [Volume 8](vol-08.md). Xbox can thin. Azure will not. None of the four is a cloud. Writing their end state as "all become losers under NVIDIA" is too full. The rivals are still here today. There is no official "already wiped out" data on CPUs, phone SoCs, or x86 attach. Do not write who exits in which year.

Game-card supply yields: see [Volume 2](vol-02.md) and [Volume 32](vol-32.md). That is not about the four counter-attacking. It does decide that their downstream is squeezed first. The first wave squeezes PCs, commercial PCs, general-purpose servers, phones, plus security and the channel. It does not squeeze Apple first.

## 9. Who can wait, who cannot

The clock that can wait is years and a decade. Intelligent Cloud is still growing. NVIDIA data center FY26 $193,737M. Foundry and memory are pulled by the coach. States decide on licenses and safety stock. The clock that cannot wait is quarterly shipments and shelf turns. When leading-edge process, packaging, and HBM feed a 71%–75% data-center margin first, consumer cards are already official "limited supply." On the same kit, those who cannot wait are squeezed first.

Among the four: Apple can wait; Qualcomm's flagship can wait a while; AMD can wait at the inference-cabinet door and still has not taken the right to define the training cabinet; Intel's foundry clock is longer, and as long as accelerators are still in the filing they have not been wiped out. Downstream mid- and low-end phones, channel PCs, and security modules have no $119.0B-class supply commitment. The queue does not reach them. This pass has no security or channel shipment stats. The mechanism can be written. Do not invent a name list.

The five interrupts: see [Volume 8](vol-08.md). The docket: see [Volume 11](vol-11.md).

## 10. Testable markers and gaps

Testable, leave empty if there is no number: whether Intel / AMD accelerator-compute revenue is still in the filings; whether the next-generation console APU is still AMD; whether consumer notebooks can still ship a non-NVIDIA CPU design in volume; whether OEM AI servers are down to a certified list only; whether TSMC's first-customer share keeps moving off 19%; whether supply commitments keep rising; whether justice.gov shows *United States v. NVIDIA*; whether France issues a statement of objections; whether the EU shows an Article 101/102 opening.

What cannot be treated as fact: a joint counter-attack by the four; PTX 95%; AMD lagging 30%–50%; a CoWoS-by-customer 14%/8%; flagship SoC purchase prices; a 40-month replacement cycle; latest full gross margins for the four (not opened file by file in this pass); prepayment dollars split to TSMC or 2nm; DOJ already having sued; a 10%-of-annual-turnover fine about to be issued; the White House already having written a no-split order. A backdoor is an allegation. It does not enter the fact layer. A privileged position (training has to pass the stack and the cabinet) is structure.

This volume does not write the four as a counter-alliance about to succeed, and it does not write them as only able to be wiped out. Each is still there. Bid and slots sit with the first name. Regulation is still at inquiry. National-security files explain a political constraint, not a no-split order.

## 15. How the first siphon lands on the four's downstream

Game-card supply yields: see [Volume 2](vol-02.md). The first wave does not squeeze Apple. It squeezes PCs, commercial PCs, general-purpose servers, phones, plus security and the channel. Security, the channel, and white-box have no four-cloud mixed-year spend, and even less a supply-commitment prepayment. The queue does not reach them. This pass has no security or channel shipment stats. Do not name who dies.

Servers have to be split: AI cabinets are swelling. There is no "shipments already wiped out" series for general-purpose servers. CPU down-binning can be written. Wipe-out cannot. Grace shows they also do not want to leave the CPU to a rival. Console APUs, phone SoCs, and x86 attach are all still there. Dells shipping certified systems is a cabinet slice that has already happened. Consumer notebooks assembled to drawings is not done. The wafer foundry is still TSMC.

The four sit in different places on this siphon. Apple's device loop can keep everyday intelligence on-device and can bear wafer price-up for a stretch. Qualcomm writing the price-up into the SoC is using phone profit to yield wafers to the cloud. AMD wants a cloud substitute, yet it bids against Apple and Broadcom for the same packaging slice. Intel wants foundry sovereignty. That clock is longer than a consumer shelf, and slower than the right to define the training cabinet. A joint counter-attack does not hold, because they bid against one another for material.
