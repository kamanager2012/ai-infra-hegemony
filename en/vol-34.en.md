# Volume 34: Restructuring the Global Semiconductor Supply Chain — Equipment, Materials, Manufacturing, and Geopolitical Redundancy

Chinese source edition: [vol-34.md](../vol-34.md)

> Retrieval and revision cutoff: **2026-08-27**. Frontier AI depends on an industrial chain that cannot be replicated quickly. This volume separates equipment, materials, manufacturing, qualification, and geographic redundancy rather than treating “domestic capacity” as one variable.

## 1. Behind the AI chip is a long industrial chain

The physical supply chain for a frontier AI system includes at least:

- semiconductor equipment;
- wafers and specialty materials;
- lithography;
- foundry manufacturing;
- HBM;
- advanced packaging;
- substrates;
- testing.

A chip designer therefore does not determine supply security alone.

The system depends on several industrial layers whose replacement clocks can be measured in years rather than product quarters.

## 2. ASML: a structural concentration point in advanced lithography

EUV lithography is unusually concentrated.

ASML's strategic position rests on a system that combines:

- optics;
- light sources;
- precision stages;
- metrology;
- installation and service.

These capabilities cannot be reproduced simply by subsidizing the construction of a new fab.

A leading-edge foundry depends not only on a building and a process recipe, but also on reliable equipment delivery and a long-duration service ecosystem.

## 3. High-NA matters as a technology path, not as one headline tool price

High-NA EUV is one of the key technology paths for future leading-edge lithography.

Its strategic importance should be evaluated through:

- tool availability;
- throughput;
- process adoption;
- yield learning;
- customer deployment.

Public reports about per-tool prices are useful for understanding capital intensity.

But without a consistent official price list, the sticker price should not become the central factual anchor.

The more important question is whether enough tools can be delivered, installed, qualified, and used productively at scale.

## 4. Japanese materials form another layer of less-visible concentration

Advanced semiconductor manufacturing depends on materials such as:

- silicon wafers;
- photoresists;
- mask blanks;
- specialty chemicals;
- polishing materials.

Japanese suppliers have deep capabilities in several of these categories.

The barrier to entry is not simply chemical-production capacity.

It also includes:

- purity;
- defect control;
- customer qualification;
- process stability.

A nominally similar material cannot be swapped into an advanced process without proving that it preserves yield and reliability.

## 5. Why material qualification creates strategic risk

Leading-edge processes are highly sensitive to contamination and defect variation.

A material can have a similar nominal composition while differing in:

- particle contamination;
- uniformity;
- defect density;
- long-term stability.

Those differences can affect yield.

The real cost of a supply interruption is therefore not merely the price of replacing the material.

It can require the fab to **requalify** a new supplier or formulation.

That process can take quarters or longer and can become a hidden source of supply-chain fragility.

## 6. What industrial policy can and cannot solve

The United States, Europe, Japan, and other economies are using industrial policy to expand:

- local fab capacity;
- advanced packaging;
- equipment and materials investment;
- supply-chain resilience.

These policies can:

- reduce geographic single-point risk;
- increase local manufacturing;
- attract capital and talent.

They cannot instantly reproduce:

- decades of yield learning;
- supplier ecosystems;
- customer qualification history;
- engineering density.

The key distinction is:

> **subsidy ≠ immediate supply-chain independence**

Industrial capability is accumulated through repeated production and qualification, not only through capital spending.

## 7. Geographic redundancy raises cost

Greater localization and redundancy can require:

- duplicated fabs;
- duplicated inventory;
- lower initial utilization;
- qualification expense;
- government incentives.

Resilience therefore carries an efficiency cost.

The semiconductor system can gradually move from:

> **lowest-cost global optimization**

toward:

> **higher-cost strategic redundancy**

That can raise system cost while lowering disruption risk.

## 8. The real meaning of TSMC geographic diversification

TSMC's overseas expansion can reduce part of the geographic concentration in front-end manufacturing.

But a complete manufacturing cluster also depends on:

- advanced packaging;
- supplier ecosystems;
- local workforce;
- power;
- water;
- operating experience.

A fab by itself does not reproduce the full Taiwan manufacturing ecosystem.

Geographic diversification is therefore a gradual process of rebuilding several connected layers, not a one-time fab relocation.

## 9. China's supply-chain localization must also be evaluated layer by layer

China's push to localize:

- equipment;
- materials;
- foundry;
- memory;
- packaging;

follows the same strategic logic of reducing external dependency.

But maturity differs by layer.

The useful evaluation criteria are:

- performance;
- yield;
- scale;
- qualification;
- cost.

A single “localization rate” cannot capture these differences.

A high substitution rate in one mature-node material does not imply equivalent closure in advanced lithography, HBM, or frontier packaging.

## 10. AI demand accelerates the supply-chain restructuring cycle

AI demand has raised the strategic value of advanced manufacturing.

Governments and firms are therefore more willing to pay for:

- redundancy;
- reserve capacity;
- local production;
- long-duration contracts.

AI infrastructure is becoming not only a technology cycle but also an **industrial-policy cycle**.

The competition increasingly includes the ability to preserve continuity under disruption, not simply minimize unit manufacturing cost.

## 11. Current judgment

Over the next decade, the semiconductor supply chain is more likely to show:

- continued concentration in core technologies;
- greater regional redundancy;
- more government involvement;
- higher cost;
- greater resilience.

The strategic objective is not complete autarky.

It is:

> **maintaining enough system-level continuity that a disruption in one region or one supplier does not stop the entire frontier AI stack.**
