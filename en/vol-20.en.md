# Volume 20: Strategic Implications — How Firms, Capital, and States Should Respond

Chinese source edition: [vol-20.md](../vol-20.md)

> Retrieval and revision cutoff: **2026-08-27**. The strategic recommendations in this volume are derived from mechanisms established in the preceding chapters. They are not a recommendation to bet on one company or one fixed end state.

## 1. Strategy should be built from mechanisms

The first nineteen volumes converge on three testable mechanisms:

1. value capture is rising in scarce layers;
2. system-stack competition is replacing single-chip competition;
3. AI demand is reshaping memory, packaging, power, and capital allocation.

For firms, investors, and states, the strategic objectives are therefore similar:

- reduce dependency;
- increase optionality;
- secure scarce resources;
- improve utilization.

The practical question is not “who wins AI?”

It is:

> **How do you preserve the ability to operate when the scarce layer, system winner, or capital regime changes?**

## 2. Firms: do not equate AI capability with one supplier

The most important infrastructure principle for enterprise AI is **portability**.

Useful capabilities include:

- multi-model routing;
- multi-cloud deployment;
- data portability;
- open interfaces;
- workload benchmarking;
- cost observability.

The thing worth locking in is:

> **a workflow that creates measurable business value**

—not a model API for its own sake.

A workflow can survive model substitution if its data, evaluation, integration, and business logic are portable.

## 3. Firms: manage AI through Software Unit Economics

Each AI workflow should be evaluated through a relationship such as:

```text
Unit Economics
=
Revenue or Productivity Value
- Inference
- Retrieval
- Storage
- Tool / API Cost
- Human Review
```

Without a measurable unit economic, adoption can grow while profit does not.

Useful operational metrics include:

- task-completion rate;
- cost per completed task;
- human time saved;
- error and review cost;
- retention or revenue impact.

The strategic objective is not maximum model usage.

It is reliable economic output per workflow.

## 4. Firms: match compute commitments to real demand

Long-duration reserved compute can reduce supply risk.

It can also create a large fixed obligation.

A more disciplined approach is:

- **high-certainty workload:** use longer commitments;
- **uncertain workload:** preserve flexible cloud, spot, or multi-provider access;
- **latency-sensitive workload:** place compute close to production;
- **model experimentation:** preserve portability.

A firm should not over-reserve simply because “compute may be scarce.”

Capacity duration should match workload certainty.

## 5. Firms: memory and bandwidth belong in procurement decisions

AI-system procurement should not be reduced to accelerator count.

It should also evaluate:

- HBM capacity;
- HBM bandwidth;
- interconnect;
- host memory;
- storage;
- networking;
- power.

Algorithmic optimization can reduce memory demand.

It does not remove bandwidth and data-movement constraints from system design.

A cheaper accelerator configuration can still be a poor system if the memory and interconnect path is wrong for the workload.

## 6. Capital: invest by economic layer, not by the “AI” label

AI-related assets should be separated into at least four classes.

### 6.1 Platform assets

Control system definition, software, and customer ecosystems.

### 6.2 Scarce manufacturing assets

Foundry, HBM, and advanced packaging.

### 6.3 Infrastructure assets

Powered land, grids, cooling, and data centers.

### 6.4 Application assets

Depend on workflow economics, distribution, and customer value.

These asset classes have different:

- risk;
- capital intensity;
- duration;
- profit cycles.

They should not be valued as one homogeneous “AI exposure.”

## 7. Capital: rising CapEx is not automatically bullish

High CapEx can indicate:

- demand growth;
- entry barriers;
- future capacity.

It can also create:

- depreciation;
- overbuild;
- low utilization;
- stranded assets.

The AI-infrastructure cycle therefore has to be read through:

- CapEx;
- utilization;
- revenue;
- free cash flow;
- supply commitments;
- clearing prices.

Capital intensity is valuable only when the asset base earns adequate returns.

## 8. States: advanced-manufacturing policy cannot stop at the fab

A state that subsidizes only a leading-edge logic fab while neglecting:

- HBM;
- advanced packaging;
- substrates;
- power;
- grids;
- talent;
- software migration;

does not create a complete frontier AI stack.

The unit of national competition has moved from:

> “Does the country have an advanced fab?”

to:

> **Can the country sustainably deploy a complete frontier system?**

Industrial policy therefore has to become system policy.

## 9. States: power policy is now part of semiconductor policy

Roughly **120 kW-class rack** power, together with separately sourced project/contract paths such as the 835 MW restart PPA, the revised PPA **up to 1.92 GW**, and the Google/Kairos fleet **up to 500 MWe by 2035**, makes power a component of compute policy. Project capacity must not be relabeled as live AI load.

A coherent industrial policy therefore has to connect:

- chip incentives;
- grid planning;
- generation;
- interconnection;
- data-center permitting.

Otherwise the economy can produce or buy chips that cannot be energized at scale.

Power planning and semiconductor planning now interact directly.

## 10. States: migration capability is a strategic asset

An economy dependent on only one software stack is more exposed to supply disruption and geopolitical fragmentation.

States and large enterprises therefore benefit from supporting:

- open compiler/runtime systems;
- multi-architecture education;
- portable model formats;
- interoperable networking;
- cross-stack benchmarking.

The objective is not to reject the leading platform.

It is to reduce **single-point dependency**.

## 11. Policy implications for China's parallel stack

China's long-run frontier competitiveness depends on simultaneous progress in:

- advanced logic;
- HBM;
- packaging;
- interconnect;
- software;
- power.

Single-point substitution does not create a complete system.

Algorithmic efficiency can raise the effective output of constrained hardware.

It cannot replace every missing physical layer.

The strategic goal is therefore system closure, not one domestic-content percentage.

## 12. Current strategic conclusion

For firms:

> **Increase workload portability, prove unit economics first, and only then lock long-duration compute.**

For capital:

> **Separate platform economics, manufacturing scarcity, infrastructure duration, and application unit economics.**

For states:

> **Treat semiconductors, memory, packaging, software, and power as one system policy.**

AI-infrastructure competition is ultimately not about who owns the most GPUs.

It is about:

> **who can repeatedly convert scarce physical resources into highly utilized, portable, cash-generating systems.**
