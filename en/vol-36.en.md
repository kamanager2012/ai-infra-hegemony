## 1. The question this volume answers

Capital markets are not being asked whether AI matters. The numbers visible in August 2026 are already hard: NVIDIA one year of free cash $96,575 million, one quarter of operating income $53,536 million, supply commitments up from $95.2B to $119.0B in three months; mixed-year four-cloud group-wide capex lifted from about $211.9–213.9B to about $354.0–356.5B. This volume asks only three harder things.

First, how the duration of this spending and the depreciation clock rewrite valuation. The seller almost never carries four-year server depreciation. The buyer does. Using the same growth-stock multiple on both sides mislabels a light-asset seller's profit onto a heavy-asset buyer's balance sheet.

Second, on which layer profit stops. The judgment's spine is a margin monopoly, not a share monopoly. Chips, packaging, and memory already collect scarce-slice rent. Clouds have to turn net income back into halls. Models and applications still pay that rent. If allocation cannot tell these three layers apart, the supercycle is only a narrative.

Third, whether ultra-long money buys an equity story or physical scarcity — power, packaging, and interconnection rights. The title writes "sovereign funds enter." The draft gave no holdings, no committed amounts, and no project list for any sovereign fund. Among public sources opened this pass, what can be nailed is the asset-class mix on Norges Bank Investment Management's site, and NVIDIA's own 10 August 2026 memorandum of understanding on a third-party compute-financing platform. The latter names Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR. It is not a sovereign-fund holdings table. The mobilization figure "over $500 billion over time" is an official target sentence. The memorandum still awaits definitive agreements. Do not write it as already deployed.

The engine is not a China-consumption refill, and not "priced too high, therefore it collapses." The judgment and the deep dives already wrote: demand pushes profit, profit pushes prepayments, prepayments lock capacity and HBM, rivals' costs rise, deliveries slow, margins thin, and they have even less money to bundle long contracts and switch stacks. Gross margin near 75% is bidding power. The risk is that this acceleration is not interrupted.

## 3. Mechanism (physics / commerce / geopolitics)

### 3.1 A margin monopoly, not a share monopoly

Analysis added. The margin-monopoly control is in [Volume 1](vol-01.md). This volume uses it only to set valuation: whoever can outbid cabinet-level gross margin has bidding power. The consideration for a neutrality pledge is the second name putting up a prepayment no smaller than the first name's increment, and bundling software and memory long contracts with it. All three are still undone.

### 3.2 Prepayments buy the slot

The 10-K itself admits prepaid manufacturing and capacity agreements, for current and future products. Official text names TSMC and Samsung for wafers, Hynix, Micron, and Samsung for memory, and CoWoS for packaging, but gives no dollar split. There is no line that says "paid TSMC X billion for CoWoS or 2nm first silicon." Do not invent one.

What can be nailed is the total: $95.2B to $119.0B, up $23.8B in three months, most payable through FY2027. The Q1 10-Q splits $119.0B as "$95B remaining this year, the rest through FY2031." That is the official sentence locking inventory and capacity, not a claim that the entire TSMC factory has already been bought dead. Agreements are "in certain instances cancellable, reschedulable, or adjustable." Writing "already exclusive" exceeds the evidence.

Hypothesis from here: while the margin holds, commitments keep rising. The highest-profit name can add a tranche of prepayments. TSMC's board answers to shareholders. When the first name's profit exceeds the increment from everyone else, there is no financial reason to offend the first name for the second. This is not an administrative takeover. It is prepayments buying the slot. Booked first customer 19%, second 17% is not a safe distance. Occupy the increment and next year's slots belong to the first name.

The same table also has multi-year cloud-service commitments: $27.0B at FY26 year-end, $30.0B at Q1 end. Official text: used for R&D compute. If capital markets add the $30B cloud commitment and the $119.0B supply commitment together as "foundry already bought out," the scope is wrong. The former is NVIDIA renting compute for its own R&D. The latter locks material.

### 3.3 Three clocks. Not one multiple

Analysis added. Valuation has to be read from the depreciation clock, not from the narrative. NVIDIA Q1-end net property and equipment is only $12,403 million; full-year capex is a bit over $6B. Microsoft's one-year additions to property and equipment alone are $64,551 million. The seller almost never carries four-year server depreciation. Depreciation sits on the clouds' books.

The first layer is two-to-four-year IT hardware. GPUs, HBM, and switch chips bind to model generations. Residual-value risk is highest. Buying this layer of equity buys quota and the CUDA lock, not "the long-run digital economy." NVIDIA itself almost never carries this layer's depreciation, and still collects this layer's margin.

The second layer is fifteen-year buildings and twenty-year power contracts. Substations, nuclear PPAs, liquid-cooled campuses, interconnection rights. The return curve looks like infrastructure, not seat-priced software. The right holder is capital whose liability duration is very long, not a growth fund graded every quarter. Power could interrupt expansion. It has not. Mixed-year 2025 four-cloud spending is still up to about $354.0–356.5B, which says buyers have not yet been pinned by power into a halt.

The third layer is software margin. The draft repeatedly writes traditional SaaS margin compressed from 85% to 55% because inference became cost of goods. This pass has no sample company that wrote those percentage points as an audited fact. The mechanism that can stay: seat-priced software multiples have to cut from "high increment, low marginal cost" to "usage times token cost." Whoever cannot grow usage enough to cover depreciation will be swapped, in depreciation years, for agent-native workflows. 85%→55% stays marked as draft. Do not promote it.

The most common secondary-market error is using NVIDIA FY26 revenue $215,938 million and gross margin 71.1%–75.0% to give every "AI-related" stock the same duration. The correct split: chips and the CUDA lock priced as a two-to-four-year cyclical; clouds priced on free cash against "depreciation versus incremental revenue"; nuclear, transformers, and liquid-cooled campuses priced as infrastructure. Three methods give three fair multiples. Mixing them is the micro-mechanism of a bubble.

Gross-margin gaps are also not a simple quality ranking. NVIDIA 71.1%–75.0%, TSMC 59.9%, Hynix operating margin 49%, Microsoft back-calculated net margin about 36.1% describe a light-asset scarce slice, a heavy-asset foundry, a memory cycle, and a mix of cloud plus office software — not a good-and-bad list. Using the same PEG across them will, in depreciation years, mis-kill or jointly inflate buyers and sellers. The first allocation rule is to admit they are not the same asset class.

### 3.4 Buyers are not poor. The money has another use

Microsoft FY25 net income $101,832 million. The clouds are not poor. That hundred billion has to turn back into halls. NVIDIA FY26 net income $120,067 million is earned selling the scarce slice itself. Prepayments are reproduction. It is not that the clouds have no gun. It is that cloud money has another use, and NVIDIA money is used specifically to lock the next slice of material.

Mixed-year 2025 four-cloud spending is already about $354.0–356.5B, still group-wide, not an AI line item. Amazon includes fulfillment. Meta includes Reality Labs. Alphabet includes offices and other technical facilities. Do not knock NVIDIA data-center revenue $193,737 million against four-cloud capex. Buyer and seller accounts differ. Do not write 2024 about $211.9–213.9B and 2025 about $354.0B as the same fact.

Hypothesis from here: clouds that can endure are evidenced by group capex still rising, and by the cloud segment still buying cabinets. Sony can thin into a software game brand. Azure will not. Microsoft will not shrink into a game studio. Writing both names as the same sentence "become a software brand" is rejected by this hard number.

### 3.5 The light-asset seller buys back and adds commitments at once

FY26 NVIDIA free cash $96,575 million, returned $41.1B to shareholders, and left supply commitments at $95.2B. Q1 returned about another $20B, commitments rose to $119.0B, the board separately authorized $80B of buybacks, and the dividend moved from $0.01 to $0.25 per share. The light-asset seller's cash cycle is: collect cabinet cash, lock next-generation material, send leftover cash back to shareholders. The heavy-asset buyer's cash cycle is: borrow or earn, build halls, take depreciation.

The same quarter's net income $58,321 million includes $15.9B of equity marks. Official non-GAAP net income $45,548 million. If capital markets use $58.3B of net income to extrapolate "one quarter can lock another round of capacity," the numerator is wrong. The judgment wrote: bid for capacity against operating income $53,536 million. This draft follows that line.

Q1 inventory $25,797 million, up from $21,403 million sequentially. Commitments up, inventory up, buybacks up — three things at once. This is not "already destocking," and not "already bought dead." What can be checked: whether supply commitments add again next quarter or stop for the first time; whether inventory keeps rising or comes down with shipments.

### 3.6 Packaging compute as an asset can scramble the clocks

The 10 August 2026 press release is the nearest official action this volume's title can attach to in public markets. NVIDIA wants independent compute-financing platforms with six institutions, speaking of compute as investable infrastructure, stressing counterparties on the CUDA stack, aiming to mobilize over $500 billion of third-party capital over time. Official text at the same time: memorandum of understanding, still awaiting definitive agreements.

Analysis added. The mechanism matters more than the dollar figure. If it is done, long-duration insurance money, pensions, and some sovereign money may no longer "enter" only by buying tech stocks, but by writing debt or leases directly against cabinets and campuses. That looks like the title's second layer. The risk is also here: residual value on first-layer hardware is two to four years; second-layer buildings and power are fifteen. Packaging GPU residual value as "an asset like a toll road" and selling it to ultra-long liabilities is welding two clocks back together. If the weld holds, depreciation moves off cloud books onto special-purpose vehicles, cloud free cash looks better, and the seller ships another tranche. If it does not, recovery on those vehicles cracks first, then transmits into buyer commitments and seller slots.

$500 billion is a mobilization target, not already raised, already deployed, already capitalized. This volume does not write "Wall Street has already put up $500B" as fact. What can be checked: whether definitive agreements are signed; whether a first public or private compute bond is priced; whether residual-value risk sits with the user, the platform, or NVIDIA.

The six names are asset managers and a bank, not the annual reports of Norway, Singapore, Abu Dhabi, or Riyadh. If sovereign funds are only anonymous limited partners behind these platforms, public markets cannot see the holdings. For the title's "enter" to be written as already happened, fund names, project names, and amounts are still missing.

### 3.7 Sovereign funds belong on the second layer, not in first-layer trading tech

Analysis added. Sovereign funds have to match national-wealth liabilities longer than ten years: pensions, reserves, fiscal smoothing. Interconnection rights, nuclear restarts, advanced-packaging consumables, and liquid-cooling units are the assets on the same clock as fifteen-year buildings. If a sovereign fund only overweights NVIDIA and Microsoft in the secondary market, it still bought first-layer volatility, no different from a levered growth fund, and the title's "enter" has no new information.

The Norway fund is the one whose official site this pass could open. 70.2% in equities, unlisted renewable-energy infrastructure only 0.4%. The quarter's loss was decided by large U.S. technology names. This fund's public portrait right now looks more like passive first-layer exposure than a second-layer project investor. You cannot infer from that page how much NVIDIA it holds, and you cannot infer a mirror for Gulf or Singapore funds. What can be written is only the mechanism: the world's largest public sovereign-wealth mix still shares this margin monopoly mainly through equities, not through unlisted power and packaging.

Corporate pensions and insurers share ultra-long liabilities with sovereign funds, but sit under tighter solvency rules. If AI infrastructure is to become a "new core allocation," the first problem to solve is whether four-year hardware can be packaged as a qualifying long-duration asset. The answer is almost no. What can enter those liability books is still power, buildings, and permits, not GPU generations. The 10 August memorandum is exactly a try at that step. If capital-markets reconstruction happens, it should show up as more infrastructure-financing tools, not as an unlimited rise in tech-equity weights.

Watching sovereign funds in public markets means watching for direct projects, not tech-stock weights. Anyone can do the latter. The draft had no project list, so this volume writes sovereign funds as "the place they should appear," not "amounts that have already appeared." Any allocation figure for Saudi Arabia, Abu Dhabi, or Singapore was not opened, this pass, in those funds' own annual reports or official project notices to a citable degree. Leave it blank. Blog and aggregator figures of $1.2B, $2.7B, $120B commitments do not enter the fact layer.

### 3.9 FX shows only in the won table

Hynix profit is in won. Expansion tools are in dollars. True leverage at a memory maker amplifies when the currency runs the other way. ### 3.10 Allocation discipline: sell them apart. Do not bundle one narrative

Analysis added. Any position whose return depends on the next GPU shipping on time should be managed on a two-to-four-year cycle. Any position whose return depends on transformers and permits should be managed on a ten-year clock. Putting both into one "AI theme fund" is scrambling the clocks again. If the title's supercycle exists, it still has to be sold apart.

Public capital is the same mechanism in a state version. Chip acts, export controls, sovereign AI: taxpayer money driven into copying a single point. If public capital covers only the building and not power and people, the books form "subsidized, no-cash-flow" assets — the same mismatch as four-year cloud depreciation, except the payer is the taxpayer. Chip-act appropriations were not checked this pass. Do not invent amounts.

### 3.11 The accounting face of a valuation rewrite, not a slogan

Analysis added. A valuation rewrite lands on the books as only three things. First, the numerator moves from "revenue growth" to "who still collects scarce-slice rent." The revenue bulk is still on cabinets; see [Volume 1](vol-01.md). Q1 FY27 on the old cut: data-center compute $60.4B, networking $14.8B; edge $6.4B. Networking $14.8B in one quarter says rent is not collected only on GPUs. If valuation only gives a "chip-stock" multiple, it drops the referee corner and the coach corner. Second, the denominator moves from "next-quarter shipments" to "whether commitments can be rescheduled." Inside $119.0B are agreements that can be adjusted. Third, the discount rate has to move from a software low-capex assumption back to a heavy-asset depreciation and interconnection assumption. Keep pricing clouds on a software discount rate and you are treating buildings as expensed code.

R&D also has to be put back. FY26 R&D $18,497 million, free cash $96,575 million. The seller is not burning cash into its own wafer fab. It burns cash into the roadmap, the software stack, and buybacks, and uses prepayments to make other people's plants queue for it. Buyer R&D and capex are another table. Mixing them in one "tech growth" basket is, in accounting, adding two tables wrong.

Four-cloud scope itself is valuation noise. $215B is closest to a mixed-year rounding; it differs from the reproducible $211.9–213.9B by $1.1–3.1B. Magnitude matches, scope does not. Mixed-year 2025 $354.0–356.5B has already changed bins. Whoever still uses $215B to push 2026–2028 annual depreciation is telling a new story on an old base. $45–55B of annual depreciation is a template pushed from four-year hardware plus fifteen-year buildings, not a sum of four 10-K asset classes. This volume does not promote it to audited depreciation expense.

### 3.12 A buyback authorization is not proof that capacity is already locked dead

Buyback authorization remaining at Q1 end: $38.5B. On 18 May the board authorized another $80B with no expiry. Some will say: if they are still buying back at this size, capacity is already enough and the prepayment story is over. That treats two cash uses as zero-sum. Officially, the same quarter both added commitments to $119.0B and returned about $20B to shareholders. Free cash $48,554 million covers both ends. The light-asset seller's constraint is not its own plant depreciation. It is other people's packaging lines and memory stacks. Buybacks prove cash left over, not that the scarce slice has loosened.

Outlook revenue $91.0B, no assumed China data-center compute revenue, is another discipline. The China market cannot save global cabinets. Missing that slice, the margin outlook is still written at 74.9%–75.0%. Bidding power does not rest on that block. A regional bid that cannot fill the gap is visible in the outlook sentence itself.

## 4. Analysis added (scenarios, opposition, testable markers)

Analysis added. 2026 $670–750B and the incremental-revenue gap are draft forecasts. This pass adds three firms' guidance to about $490–520B and is missing Microsoft. Not an audit fact. $115.5B depreciation, $155B required increment, $35–50B current increment: scenario arithmetic.

Scenario A: the supercycle continues. Valuation is relabeled from "software multiples" to "quota rent." NVIDIA margin holds in the 71%–75% band; TSMC 59.9% and Hynix 49% are taken as the new seller midpoints. Clouds digest depreciation on ads and existing cloud; group capex does not return to the mixed-year 2024 $210B order. If the 10 August financing platforms become definitive agreements, long-duration money starts buying campuses and cabinet leases, not only chasing chip prices. If sovereign funds enter, they mainly buy nuclear, grid, and foundry-related long projects. Prepayments keep rising. The line walks past halfway on the scarce slice.

Scenario B: the depreciation test fails. Four-year hardware cannot find matching incremental revenue. Clouds cut group capex. Tech stocks kill the linked valuation of buyers and sellers. That is Volume 37's hard landing mapped onto capital markets. What cracks first is cloud free cash, not the fab quote. Supply commitments stopping for the first time, or large-scale rescheduling, would be the public signal for this path. It has not been seen. Mixed-year 2025 is still rising, not already cut. Do not write the stress test as "priced too high, therefore it collapses." The judgment's risk is that this acceleration is not interrupted: profit keeps pushing prepayments, prepayments keep locking the next generation, and the second name has even less money to bundle long contracts. Collapse conditions are two consecutive years of negative growth in buyer group capex, and NVIDIA data-center revenue leaving the FY26 $193,737 million order. Neither is a fact yet.

Scenario C: algorithm cost-down makes the incremental-revenue curve steeper than the capex curve. The draft writes DeepSeek training cost about $5.576 million. The paper was not re-checked this pass. If enterprise usage rises while token prices fall, valuation cuts from "the more expensive compute, the better" to "the cheaper a unit of intelligence, the better," and chip stocks and cloud stocks diverge. If cloud ASICs only shunt inference and do not take the first-to-market slot for training cabinets, this path is still local. Public next generations have not retreated to 5nm plus commodity memory. For scenario C to become the judgment's interrupt, the three bundled pieces are still missing: prepayments, a software stack, and memory long contracts.

Three opposing claims all exceed the evidence. Extrapolating NVIDIA's light-asset margin onto the clouds; treating 2026 $750B as already capitalized fact; writing "sovereign funds enter" as a large-scale private-equity or infrastructure allocation that has already happened, or writing the $500B mobilization target as already funded. This volume refuses all three.

- Whether NVIDIA supply commitments stay above $119.0B or fall sequentially for the first time; whether inventory stays above $25,797 million or comes down with shipments.
- Whether gross margin leaves the 71.1% to 75.0% band; whether operating income leaves the quarterly $53,536 million order. The last two are controls, not forecasts.

- Whether public information shows a sovereign fund's own announcement of a direct project investment in a nuclear PPA, a data center, or a fab. If yes, record it. If not, do not backfill from aggregators.
- Next quarter on the Norway fund site: whether unlisted renewable-energy infrastructure is still near 0.4%, or starts approaching a share that would change the portfolio clock.
