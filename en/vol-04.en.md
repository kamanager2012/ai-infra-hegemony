# Volume 04: TSMC, N2, and Advanced-Manufacturing Resource Allocation

Chinese source edition: [vol-04.md](../vol-04.md)

> Retrieval and revision cutoff: **2026-08-27**. Advanced-manufacturing competition is not reduced here to one wafer quote or one customer-share number. The relevant object is access to the next increment of complete frontier-system resources.

## 1. The real competition is not a “2 nm price”

Leading-edge manufacturing is often described through questions such as:

- who gets 2 nm first;
- how much one wafer costs.

That framing is too narrow for frontier AI.

A complete system also requires:

- leading-edge logic;
- HBM;
- advanced packaging;
- substrates;
- high-speed interconnect;
- power.

The strategic issue is therefore:

> **Who gets the next increment of scarce advanced resources, in what sequence, and with what degree of certainty?**

Node access is one part of a multi-layer allocation problem.

## 2. TSMC's 2025 factual baseline

TSMC's public 2025 disclosures include:

- approximately **15.0M 12-inch-equivalent wafer shipments**;
- annual capacity above **17M wafers**;
- 3 nm: **24%** of wafer revenue;
- 5 nm: **36%**;
- 7 nm: **14%**;
- 7 nm and below: **74%**;
- HPC platform: **58%** of revenue, up **48%** year over year;
- Smartphone platform: **29%** of revenue, with platform revenue up **11%** year over year;
- largest customer: **19% of annual revenue**;
- second-largest customer: **17%**;
- 4Q24 transcript: **advanced packaging and testing >8% of revenue**;
- 4Q25 transcript (company-corrected wording): **advanced packaging slightly above 10% of 2025 revenue**. The labels are not identical, so 8%→10% should not be treated as a perfectly continuous like-for-like series.

The source base includes TSMC's 2025 Annual Report, 4Q25 Management Report, and investor-conference materials.

Two conclusions follow.

First, HPC has become TSMC's largest platform.

Second, advanced manufacturing is highly concentrated economically while still serving many customers.

Public evidence therefore does not support the claim that TSMC has become the exclusive fab of one customer.

## 3. Customer revenue share is neither wafer share nor a same-customer time series

The 2025 largest-customer **19%** and second-largest-customer **17%** figures are annual **revenue-ranking statistics**.

They cannot be converted directly into:

- N2 wafer share;
- 3 nm wafer share;
- CoWoS share;
- HPC wafer share;
- physical wafer count.

Equally important, **annual largest / second-largest rankings must not be stitched into a same-customer longitudinal series**. A separate anonymized disclosure provides the directly comparable 2024→2025 customer series:

- Customer A: **12%→19%**;
- Customer B: **22%→17%**.

It is therefore invalid to connect a prior year's “largest customer” percentage to the later Customer A percentage and explain the result as one customer's changing share. The two disclosures answer different questions and must remain separate.

Different products also use different nodes, die sizes, wafer ASPs, and packaging intensity.

For the same reason, HPC's **58% revenue share** is not equivalent to 58% of wafer shipments.

The report therefore uses **AHICR — Advanced/HPC Incremental Capacity Capture Ratio** to analyze forward competition.

The question is how much of the **new scarce increment** each buyer captures, rather than treating historical revenue share as future physical allocation.

## 4. Advanced packaging is a first-party confirmed bottleneck

TSMC management has repeatedly described advanced-packaging tightness.

In 2024 Q4, management said capacity was **very tight** and could not meet all customer need.

In 2026 Q2, management said packaging capacity was tight enough to **limit customers' growth**.

Those statements are more important than unverified monthly capacity narratives because they come from the supplier itself.

TSMC does not publish a customer-level CoWoS split.

TrendForce's estimate that NVIDIA represented more than 60% of 2025 CoWoS demand should therefore remain **B-grade institutional research**, not be presented as a TSMC allocation disclosure.

## 5. 5.37 wafers per rack is only a packaging stress test

The report uses **5.37 CoWoS wafers per rack** as a **D-grade geometry stress test**.

Its role is to estimate the order of magnitude of advanced-packaging pressure from rack-scale deployment.

It is not a measure of:

- TSMC's full-fab capacity;
- actual Blackwell shipments;
- CoWoS yield;
- customer allocation.

It must not be divided into TSMC's approximately 15M annual wafer shipments.

Those figures describe different physical pools.

Keeping those pools separate is essential to avoid order-of-magnitude errors.

## 6. N2 matters because it represents the next increment of frontier logic

N2 is strategically important not merely because of transistor density.

It represents a new generation of leading-edge logic capacity for frontier systems.

The real competitive questions are:

- who enters volume production earlier;
- who receives enough capacity to matter;
- who can match that logic with HBM and packaging;
- who can maintain yield and delivery;
- who can convert node migration into system-level performance.

“Getting N2” is therefore not victory by itself.

A system that lacks HBM, advanced packaging, interconnect, or power still cannot be delivered at scale.

## 7. How a supplier is likely to allocate scarce advanced capacity

A foundry cannot rationally allocate capacity using only one variable.

Relevant commercial considerations can include:

- price;
- committed volume;
- contract duration;
- prepayments;
- customer credit quality;
- utilization certainty;
- roadmap stability;
- customer diversification;
- geopolitical risk.

The appropriate mechanism is therefore not:

> “TSMC has no reason to upset its largest customer.”

It is:

> **A buyer with large long-duration orders, strong credit, substantial prepayments, and a stable roadmap can improve its bargaining position for the next increment—while TSMC still has incentives to preserve customer diversification and manage geopolitical concentration risk.**

This is a probabilistic commercial mechanism, not customer determinism.

## 8. Why Samsung and other foundries still matter

TrendForce's 2026 Q1 foundry-revenue estimates were approximately:

- TSMC: **72%**;
- Samsung: **6.5%**;
- SMIC: **5.1%**.

These are foundry revenue shares, not frontier-AI capacity shares.

Samsung's existence means advanced foundry supply is not theoretically a one-company market.

But practical substitutability depends on more than node availability:

- node performance;
- yield;
- packaging;
- HBM ecosystem;
- customer qualification;
- software and system integration.

“A second advanced foundry exists” and “a fully substitutable second frontier-AI supply stack exists” are different claims.

## 9. Current judgment

TSMC's strategic position rests on three simultaneous facts:

1. it has a very large share of leading-edge logic;
2. HPC is its largest platform;
3. advanced packaging remains a publicly confirmed growth constraint.

The most important forward variables are therefore not one quarter's customer revenue shares.

They are:

- customer structure in N2/N2P/A16 incremental capacity;
- the speed of advanced-packaging expansion;
- HBM supply;
- cloud custom silicon competing for the same increment;
- whether a second supply system can provide **logic + packaging + HBM** together.

Advanced-manufacturing competition is moving from:

> **who owns the most advanced node?**

toward:

> **who can reliably obtain every scarce layer required to deliver the next frontier system?**
