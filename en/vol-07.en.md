# Volume 07: NVIDIA Vertical Expansion and AI Factory Control

Chinese source edition: [vol-07.md](../vol-07.md)

> Retrieval and revision cutoff: **2026-08-27**. NVIDIA's vertical expansion is analyzed here as an expansion of system definition and interfaces, not as proof that every adjacent profit pool has already been internalized.

## 1. The direction of expansion: from accelerator vendor to system platform

NVIDIA's expansion is not primarily a story of acquiring more hardware companies.

It is a story of expanding the part of the AI system that NVIDIA defines.

The current platform boundary spans:

- GPUs and CPUs;
- NVLink and NVSwitch;
- networking;
- rack-scale reference architecture;
- liquid-cooling requirements;
- CUDA and runtime layers;
- inference software;
- AI Enterprise;
- DGX and cloud services;
- model-related products.

This is **vertical expansion through interfaces and system-definition power**.

## 2. Moving the sales unit upward increases system control

In the accelerator-card era, OEMs could design much of the server around a GPU.

Rack-scale systems impose requirements across:

- power delivery;
- cooling;
- fabric;
- cable topology;
- rack layout;
- firmware;
- the software stack.

The OEM role can therefore move from “define the whole product around a component” toward “integrate and certify a reference system.”

That does not mean Dell, Lenovo, HPE, and other OEMs have lost product capability.

It means the NVIDIA reference architecture increasingly constrains the engineering space in which the final system is built.

## 3. Networking is now part of the platform

NVIDIA's Data Center business increasingly combines compute and networking.

High-speed interconnect matters because it can:

- improve multi-GPU scaling;
- reduce communication bottlenecks;
- make rack and pod architecture more unified;
- tie accelerator purchases more closely to network architecture.

Platform competition is therefore moving beyond a simple “GPU plus third-party network” model.

The product increasingly resembles a complete **AI Factory architecture** in which compute and interconnect are co-designed.

## 4. Strategic role of DGX Cloud

DGX Cloud should not be evaluated only by whether it already represents a separately disclosed large revenue pool.

It has at least four strategic functions:

1. showing end users the full NVIDIA system architecture directly;
2. generating workload and deployment feedback;
3. lowering the adoption barrier for the full NVIDIA stack;
4. creating another channel and bargaining option alongside hyperscalers.

DGX Cloud is therefore best understood today as a **platform extension and channel-control instrument**.

Without a separate revenue disclosure, it should not be described as a second major profit pool that has already replaced hyperscaler distribution.

## 5. AI Enterprise, NIM, NeMo, and model-layer products

NVIDIA has extended its software portfolio into:

- NVIDIA AI Enterprise;
- NIM;
- NeMo;
- Nemotron;
- deployment blueprints.

These products can increase platform stickiness by reducing:

- integration effort;
- deployment friction;
- migration incentives.

Their current financial role is better described as:

> **making the hardware/system stack easier to deploy, harder to leave, and more useful to enterprise customers**

rather than estimating an undisclosed software share of system profit.

## 6. Automotive and Physical AI extend the platform into new domains

Automotive, robotics, and industrial AI are important directions of platform expansion.

The strategic logic resembles Data Center:

- hardware architecture;
- runtime;
- simulation;
- model deployment;
- developer tools;

are bundled into a platform.

But strategic importance and current financial scale are different questions.

Automotive and robotics can matter greatly to the long-run platform strategy without already contributing Data Center-level revenue or profit.

Their progress should therefore be evaluated through:

- product presence;
- design wins;
- revenue;
- ecosystem adoption;
- migration cost.

## 7. How an asset-light platform can shape a capital-intensive supply chain

NVIDIA does not own the leading-edge logic fabs, HBM fabs, or most advanced-packaging manufacturing capacity on which its systems depend.

It can still influence supplier expansion through:

- long-duration commitments;
- prepayments;
- roadmap coordination;
- reference designs;
- volume visibility.

This creates an **asset-light orchestration** model:

> platform retains high-value system-definition power  
> → manufacturing CapEx remains with foundries, memory vendors, and infrastructure providers  
> → platform uses cash flow and forward orders to secure future resources

That is one reason platform capital efficiency can differ sharply from an IDM model.

## 8. Limits to vertical expansion

Expanding into a system platform does not mean NVIDIA will control every adjacent industry.

Important constraints include:

- hyperscaler self-designed system stacks;
- open software layers;
- alternative networking;
- regulatory intervention;
- customer multi-sourcing;
- power constraints;
- China's parallel stack.

If several of these forces strengthen together, NVIDIA can remain the leading system platform while still facing meaningful limits on further industrial control.

Vertical expansion and complete vertical integration are different states.

## 9. Current judgment

As of **2026**, NVIDIA has clearly moved beyond the role of an accelerator company toward an AI system platform.

The most defensible changes are:

- the sales unit has moved upward;
- networking and compute are more tightly integrated;
- software/runtime coverage has expanded;
- system reference architecture has become more influential;
- model and cloud products extend the platform upward.

The decisive question for the next stage is:

> **Can NVIDIA continue converting system-definition power into durable access to supply, customers, and workloads?**

That is the mechanism to watch—not an unsupported claim that every neighboring profit pool has already been absorbed.
