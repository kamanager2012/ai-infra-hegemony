# Volume 11: Antitrust, industrial policy, and the new power structure in the AI era (sovereign-AI statutes and the limit of antitrust)

Chinese original: [vol-11.md](vol-11.md)

Rent and prepayments are in [Volume 8](vol-08.md). Docket search, statutory ceilings, and which cell the three lines sit in are in [Volume 31](vol-31.md). This volume writes only what regulation can still split when the four corners sit together.

## 1. The question this volume answers

When athlete, referee, coach, and model player sit in the same legal person, what can classic antitrust still split? This volume answers the power structure. It does not retell the gross margin, and it does not invent a table of cases that do not exist. The deepening already named the point: regulation hits contracts; it does not hit the CoWoS queue or the HBM conversion. The conflict between the model player (NIM) and the athlete (selling cabinets to model firms) is structural. Do not invent statutes. Do not write backdoors.

Four things must be split first. First, the inquiries are real; complaints and fines have not landed. As of 2026-08-26, justice.gov has no *United States v. NVIDIA* complaint; France has not issued a statement of objections; the EU has no formal Article 101/102 opening. Second, what can change the rules overnight is a split, a forced-open CUDA, or a ban on tied supply, not a fine. Third, White House NSM-25, Commerce BIS, and DoD have already written advanced chips and large compute clusters as national security. The files prove "written as national security," not "already decided not to split." Fourth, FY26 data center $193.7B is NVIDIA's own reported revenue, the compute rent the world paid; four-cloud capex is group-wide equipment purchases and cannot be added into "AI hardware $215B."

This volume uses the judgment and [Volume 11](vol-11.md). Search cutoff 2026-08-26. The numbers are current. What follows from them is a hypothesis from the present. The draft's 80% monopoly, $40B single fab, 40%–45% cabinet software premium, and NVLink Switch gross margin above 85% are all dropped to draft claims. They do not enter the fact layer.

## 3. Push the mechanism (physics / commerce / geopolitics)

**The four corners sitting together are the power outside the relevant-market definition.** The judgment: NVIDIA is athlete, referee, and coach at once, and it also trains models. The FY26 full table is in [Volume 1](vol-01.md). This volume does not reopen it. The referee is CUDA, NVLink, and the cabinet standard. The coach is the Hopper-to-Blackwell-to-Rubin mix. The model player is Nemotron, NeMo, and NIM. The revenue bulk is still on cabinets. The sales unit moved from the chip to the cabinet.

Added analysis. Antitrust habitually cuts by relevant market: accelerators one slice, networking one slice, software one slice, model services one slice. With the four corners in one legal person, each cut slice "has rivals": accelerators have AMD and cloud ASICs, networking has Ethernet, software has the UXL / oneAPI embryos, models have the whole open- and closed-source track. Taken together, the athlete sets the cabinet, the referee sets the interface, the coach sets who first gets CoWoS and HBM, and the model player stands next door to the training that others do on its stack. If the remedy hits contract terms on only one slice, the other three remain. That is the "new power structure": not a share number, but four powers that did not need a court merger.

**Inquiries exist. Complaints do not.** What [Volume 11](vol-11.md) nails is what public sources can check. U.S. DOJ: Reuters on 2024-08-02, citing The Information — an inquiry already open, looking at whether clouds were pressed to buy multiple products, and whether networking gear was marked up when buying a rival's chips. DOJ declined to comment to the Associated Press at the time. Bloomberg on 2024-09-03: subpoenas, from questionnaires up to binding process, written as "a step closer to a formal complaint," not already a complaint. NVIDIA statement on 2024-09-04: it asked DOJ, had not been subpoenaed, and was willing to answer regulator questions. Civil investigative demands are often called subpoenas in talk; a subpoena is what DOJ's own paper says. As of 2026-08-26, among public Antitrust Division cases on justice.gov, the 2025 complaint that can be read is *U.S. v. Hewlett Packard Enterprise Co. and Juniper Networks, Inc.* (2025-01-30). The defendant is not NVIDIA. There is no *United States v. NVIDIA Corp.* Complaint, and no Competitive Impact Statement or Proposed Final Judgment. In March 2026 Warren and Blumenthal sent a letter on the Groq license. That is a congressional inquiry, not an antitrust complaint. Conclusion: the inquiry is real. Complaints and fines have not landed. Do not write a subpoena as a split order.

**France is near the end. No statement of objections has issued.** The company wrote it itself. 2023-11-21 quarterly: the French Competition Authority collected information on graphics cards and the cloud-service-provider market; EU and Chinese regulators also asked for materials on GPU sales and supply allocation. The same Reuters piece wrote that France raided the Paris office in September. That is an inquiry, not a penalty decision. Reuters 2026-07-09: general rapporteur Umberto Berkani told reporters the inquiry is near the end. Near the end does not mean sanctions must follow. The investigation service first decides whether to issue a statement of objections; it can also close the case. If issued, the company can still reply, and the board then makes a final ruling. From 2026-07-09 to 2026-08-26, the Autorité de la concurrence site had no statement of objections or penalty decision on NVIDIA that we could read. Secondary copy writes "near a decision on whether to accuse" as "about to issue a charge." The original does not. Conclusion: the inquiry is near the end. Whether a statement of objections has issued: on the public record, no. It can also close.

**The EU sent questionnaires. No 101/102 opening. No fine.** Reuters 2024-12-06: regulators were asking rivals and customers whether NVIDIA tied products or took an unfair advantage; sources said this step could lead to a formal investigation. Reuters 2024-12-04: the same questionnaire round hung on the Run:ai merger review, not an already-opened 101/102 case. The merger itself: the Commission unconditionally approved Run:ai on 2024-12-20. NVIDIA on 2025-01-10 sued the Commission in Italy over Article 22 referral (T-15/25). Reuters 2025-02-24: that case does not affect the already-approved deal. That is a merger-jurisdiction suit, not an abuse-of-dominance fine. Capitol Forum 2026-02-11 cited sources that the Commission was still asking how to define data-center components. That is news, not a Commission *Opening of proceedings* release. Fortune 2024-07-05 quoted Vestager: chip supply is a bottleneck. That is not a regulatory action. As of 2026-08-26: no formal DG COMP opening, statement of objections, or fine on NVIDIA under 101/102 was read. Conclusion: a formal opening or a fine, no. Questionnaires, yes.

**10% of annual turnover is a statute, not a fine.** Council Regulation (EC) No 1/2003 Article 23(2): for each undertaking participating in the infringement, the fine shall not exceed 10% of its total turnover in the preceding business year. The Commission's fines page repeats that sentence. Article 7 of the same regulation is the clause that can split structure: the Commission may impose behavioral or structural remedies; structural remedies only when behavioral remedies would be equally effective or more burdensome. That is a power list, not something already used on NVIDIA. French Commercial Code L464-2: the firm-level sanction ceiling is 10% of worldwide pre-tax turnover in the highest completed accounting year, counted from the year before the conduct through the years the conduct ran. NVIDIA FY26 revenue $215,938 million. 10% is $21,593.8 million. That is the statutory ceiling times the company's own annual report, not a number issued by the Commission or the French authority. With no opening and no statement of objections, this product cannot be written as "about to issue a $21.6B fine." The U.S. Sherman Act has no "10% of annual turnover" clause. What can change the rules overnight on the U.S. side is a split, an injunction, or forced interoperability, not copying the EU ceiling across.

**Once national security writes advanced compute as a license item, a split is harder to land.** A split is doable in doctrine. After the files below write the same slice of silicon as national security, it is harder to land administratively. The files only prove "written as national security." They do not prove "already decided not to split." White House 2024-10-24 first AI national-security memorandum NSM-25: frontier AI has major implications for national security and foreign policy; developing advanced AI systems requires large volumes of advanced chips; the memorandum calls for improving the security and diversity of the chip supply chain, and next-generation government supercomputers should take AI into account. Commerce BIS 2022-10-07 interim final rule (Federal Register 2022-10-13, 87 FR 62186): controls on advanced-computing integrated circuits, computer commodities containing them, and certain semiconductor-manufacturing items. 2025-01-15 interim final rule (AI Diffusion IFR): brings global diffusion of the most advanced AI models and large-scale advanced-computing IC clusters under export control, to protect U.S. national security and foreign-policy interests. NVIDIA's own FY26 10-K: as of FY26 year-end, the company has been effectively blocked from China's data-center compute market. Same 10-K: the January 2025 AI Diffusion IFR would have imposed a global license on H200, GB200, and GB300; in May 2025 the U.S. government announced it would withdraw and write a replacement, scope unset. That is export control changing, not an antitrust close. Pentagon 2023-11-02 *Data, Analytics, and AI Adoption Strategy*: accelerate adoption of advanced AI so the warfighter keeps decision advantage; the *National Defense Strategy* is to write deterrence of China in; adversaries have publicly said they will use AI for military advantage. Conclusion: White House, Commerce, and DoD have all written advanced chips and large compute clusters as national security. That a split is hard under national security is a political constraint pushed from these files, not a signed "do-not-split" order.

**A fine is a cost. What changes the bid is a structural split.** The judgment: one quarter of Q1 FY27 operating income $53.536B. The French ceiling of about $21.6B is about 40% of one quarter of operating income, and about 18% of FY26 net income $120.067B. Added analysis. As long as there is no split, no forced-open interface, and no ban on prepayments locking capacity, the fine goes to non-operating or contingencies, and prepayments keep rising. Supply commitments $95.2B at FY26 year-end, $119.0B at Q1 FY27 end, up $23.8B in three months, most payable through FY2027. Official text locks inventory and capacity. It does not split TSMC or CoWoS. Some agreements can be rescheduled. Hypothesis from here: while the margin holds at 71% to 75%, commitments keep rising. Anyone else who wants the same material pays more, or does not get it. TSMC 2025 gross margin 59.9%, Hynix operating margin 49% — sellers already fat, and they still cannot cover the cabinet tier. No buyer, selling cabinets, can outbid that pad. TSMC has no financial reason to offend the first name for the second. A fine does not change this bid table. A split or a forced-open CUDA would. Of the five interrupts, what is done: supply commitments to $119.0B; inquiries exist, complaints do not; France is near the end and has not issued a statement of objections; the EU sent questionnaires, cleared Run:ai unconditionally, and has no 101/102 opening. 10% of annual turnover is a statutory ceiling, not a fine.

**The conflict between model player and athlete is structure, not already-split revenue.** Added analysis. The Nemotron, NeMo, and NIM product lines exist. No split revenue. Do not write token ARR. The athlete sells cabinets to model firms; the model player offers adjacent model services on the same CUDA and NIM. Others train on its stack; it sells the inference microservice next door. The conflict of interest is not a "backdoor" — backdoors do not enter the fact layer — it is: the interface the referee sets also serves its own model product. A forced-open CUDA would move this layer. Fining sales contracts alone would not. The discussion already wrote: a privileged position (training has to pass the stack) can be written as structure; a backdoor cannot. This volume complies.

**Buyers are oligopolists too. Regulation will hit its own people.** Added analysis. Four-cloud scope is in [Volume 8](vol-08.md). They are NVIDIA's largest customers, substitutes with in-house accelerators, and the gate for enterprise software distribution. If the remedy is "must supply mid-size clouds on equal terms," physics first needs surplus packaging and power. If the remedy is "clouds must not tie models to compute," it hits these firms' own cloud and advertising profits. The clouds are not poor. That hundred billion builds halls. If competition policy splits only the seller and ignores the buyer gate, the end is moving the rent from chip gross margin onto the cloud bill.

**Sovereign AI is industrial policy. Its objective function is the opposite of antitrust.** Added analysis. The sovereign narrative wants a national model, a national cluster, and national data that does not leave. It expands public procurement, allows national champions, and uses export controls to weaken foreign rivals. That is not the same objective as "lower concentration, protect consumer surplus." Export controls make it easier for the domestic champion to raise price; chip subsidies turn onshore capacity into a political asset, not a competitive increment. NVIDIA itself wrote that it has been blocked from China's data-center market. That is an administrative lock, not a French or EU statement of objections. The near gate is administrative. The far gate is everything that is not silicon. If domestic advanced packaging and HBM-class bits can already hold up a training cabinet, the administrative lock is enough to keep a closed loop. If not, the lock only keeps the phone layer. The China market cannot save global cabinets, HBM, CoWoS, or CUDA. At home it can at most do phones. PCs and AI do not resolve. The layer is wrong. U.S. antitrust cannot save Huawei. What it splits is domestic tying and supply discrimination, not opening 2nm for SMIC, and not opening HBM for CXMT. CXMT's prospectus: 0 mentions of HBM. Once the international price holds, makers sell first to the high bidder. A statute can set a procurement list and a security review. It cannot set CoWoS fixture-hours, and it cannot set the profit motive that moves HBM wafers off commodity DRAM.

**Compute rent: $193.7B is the rent end, not four-cloud AI hardware.** Scope is in [Volume 8](vol-08.md). What can be written: the four clouds are adding group-wide capex; NVIDIA collected $193.7B of FY26 data-center revenue. What cannot: four-cloud 2024 AI hardware equals $215B; knocking mixed-year capex against data-center revenue into a "rent rate."

**Regulation hits contracts. It does not hit the CoWoS queue or the HBM conversion.** Added analysis. Classic tools hit mergers, tying, refusal to deal, and price discrimination. Power at the four corners comes from three layers at once. First layer: CUDA and PTX software switching costs. The theoretical tools to split this layer are forced interoperability, published interfaces, and a ban on retaliatory cutoff. UXL and oneAPI are embryos. They have not dug through. Second layer: NVLink and cabinet-scale liquid cooling and copper, a system that does not come apart. Splitting this layer hits the performance cliff the draft already wrote — in-cabinet bandwidth and outside-cabinet Ethernet are not the same order of magnitude. 1.8 TB/s per card and 130 TB/s at cabinet versus 800G RoCE at about 100 GB/s is a draft engineering check. This volume takes it as mechanism direction, not a courtroom measurement. Third layer: TSMC CoWoS and HBM physical quotas. Splitting the third layer, the object is not even in the same company: foundry and memory are upstream oligopolists. Packaging and test about 8% in 2024, a bit over 10% in 2025. C.C. Wei, 4Q24: CoWoS tight, cannot meet demand. 2Q26: limits customers' growth. "Sold out through 2026" at the shareholders' meeting has no official transcript. A behavioral remedy on one GPU company does not change makers' profit motive to move wafers from commodity DRAM to HBM, and it does not change TSMC's having no financial reason to offend the first name for the second. Packaging math is in [Volume 4](vol-04.md). This volume borrows one sentence: the binding line is packaging, not the antitrust hearing calendar.

## 4. Added analysis (scenarios, the other side, falsifiable indicators)

Added analysis. Push forward from the numbers now. Do not assign extra probabilities. The four-scenario probabilities and the 2036 four-axis table do not enter the fact layer.

Scenario A: behavioral remedies. Publish the interface, ban retaliation, force multi-brand bids on government clusters. The result may be a slightly looser software layer and a tighter cabinet and packaging layer — buyers switch to another compiler on the same physical stack. If CUDA is ordered to publish a subset, the migration tax falls a notch. That is not more CoWoS slots. While C.C. Wei still says packaging limits customer growth, a multi-brand bid buys a slower delivery tier.

Scenario B: structural remedies. Split the systems business, or limit cabinet tying, or force networking out of the GPU contract. If the draft 40%–45% premium truly comes from the right to define software, a split may compress the average close. If the premium is in fact a shadow price on scarce CoWoS and HBM, a split only changes who invoices: one invoice for the GPU, one for the switch; the cabinet is still the same supply commitment. The U.S. side has no 10%-of-turnover clause; what can change the rules overnight is this branch. There is no complaint now, so this branch still sits on the power list. Article 7 of Regulation 1/2003 is likewise a power in Europe, not an order already used.

Scenario C: sovereignty overruns competition. National statutes require in-country model hosting and accelerator whitelists. Concentration rises inside borders and is counted as "multipolar" on the global book. Export controls have already taken China's data-center market off NVIDIA's book. That is an administrative lock, not a restoration of competition. If the domestic closed loop cannot hold a training cabinet, the lock only keeps the phone layer. If the U.S. splits its own four corners after the national-security memorandum, it slows its own iteration. Hypothesis from the judgment: a fine is a cost; a split is hard under national security. Raised high, what lands is a settlement, a little give on interconnect, and ASICs allowed onto part of the network. The fat margin and the prepayments are not touched.

Scenario D: regulation spins. Cases open, hearings, commitments; the queue and HBM lead times do not change. Supply commitments up $23.8B in three months. Firms keep doing what the draft already advised: multi-chip elastic scheduling as self-insurance. That is not a legal victory. It is lowering exposure to single-stack rent extraction inside the regulatory limit. The investment overweight in advanced-packaging consumables, liquid cooling, nuclear, and interconnection metrics is itself a bet that "regulation cannot hit the physical short side." Money is betting against the law. Physics is harder than the statute.

Other side one: high margin equals an actionable monopoly. FY26 gross margin 71.1%, Q4 75.0%, FY25 full year 75.0% is necessary but not sufficient. The U.S. does not punish a monopoly "won by product and foresight." What you hit, if you hit, is tying, supply discrimination, and using capacity to punish people who switch stacks. That takes contracts, mail, and customer testimony. An inquiry can run for years. This volume has no such docket. Other side two: open source and ASICs already constrain. Public next-gen parts are not 5nm plus commodity DDR5. Maia 200 writes TSMC N3, HBM3e 216GB; Ironwood official 192GB HBM3E; Trainium3 writes first 3nm AI chip, HBM3e 144GB. No firm says it is going back to 5nm plus commodity DDR5 as the inference mainstay. The direction is the opposite. To beat today's cabinet you return to 3nm, HBM, and CoWoS, and you bid against 75% gross margin. The constraint is incomplete. Other side three: power and foundry are the objects that should be regulated. That would make antitrust into public-utility law, harder politically, closer to the physical short side. Power is one of the three forks. It has not won. This volume does not write nuclear operators into the 60%–75% gross-margin sentence. Other side four: $21.6B already hurts enough. Arithmetic is not a fine. One quarter of Q1 operating income $53.536B. While structure is not split, this line is protection money. Other side five: Run:ai cleared unconditionally, so the Commission thinks the software layer is clean. What cleared unconditionally is a merger, not a disclaimer on 101/102. ## 6. Of the five interrupts, what is done is the inquiry and the commitment add

The judgment listed five lines that would break it. The West actually bundles: prepayments large enough that the foundry cannot recognize only one buyer; software bound into one stack; memory longs that leave a line for commodity bits. Miss one check and you are still picked off one by one. Cloud ASICs take training off the cabinet, not just shunt inference. Power stops the buildout. The East stands up a second bid. A structural split or a forced-open CUDA. None of the five is done.

What is done: supply commitments to $119.0B; inquiries exist, complaints do not; France is near the end and has not issued a statement of objections; the EU sent questionnaires, cleared Run:ai unconditionally, and has no 101/102 opening. Fail them and the line walks past half of the scarce slice. The window is the few years before the next first-to-market node is locked by commitments. After that, a second name that bundles is still asking for residual. Booked first customer 19% is not a safe distance. Occupy the increment and next year's slots belong to the first name.

China's stack is in [Volume 12](vol-12.md). U.S. antitrust cannot save Huawei. What it splits is domestic tying, not opening 2nm for SMIC.

The engine has not changed. Demand pushes profit, profit pushes prepayments, prepayments lock capacity and HBM, rival costs rise, deliveries slow, profits thin, they have even less money to bind longs and switch stacks, and share widens again. Antitrust slows the step and makes it pay protection money. It does not split the machine that prints the profit — that is a hypothesis from here, not a signed policy. What can stop this rent is still the same few lines: the application end never makes money and capex stops itself; the West bundles; ASICs take training; power stops the buildout; a split or an open CUDA; the East stands up a second bid. None of these is done. Doing the Eastern one is only a second gravity. It cannot save the allocation of global cabinets.

## 7. The three powers are not in the same court

It is enough to collect the "new power structure" into three powers. Do not invent another agency name. Added analysis. The first power is whoever defines the system: whoever specifies in-cabinet interconnect, the compiler, and cabinet turnkey collects that tier of system-layer premium. The draft 40%–45% is an engineering estimate. This volume keeps only the direction that "the right to define is worth money." It does not write the range as audited. The second power is whoever allocates the molecules: whoever queues CoWoS and HBM decides which class of box takes the downstream cost shock. The first wave is PCs, commercial PCs, general-purpose servers, and phones, paying a residual tax, not stopping. The third power is whoever approves the electrons: whoever issues interconnection and export licenses can jump the queue or take a whole market off the board. NVIDIA itself wrote that it has been blocked from China's data-center market. That is the third power already used once.

Antitrust is good at chasing the first power's contract terms: tying, supply discrimination, questionnaires, and subpoenas. Industrial policy is good at subsidizing the second power's onshore plants, and at using export controls to cut the second power's buyers into two price books. Energy regulation and export control are good at reviewing the third power's rates and licenses. The three powers are not in the same court. That is the institutional meaning of the regulatory limit. This volume does not recommend merging regulators. It only opposes covering three powers with a single statute title. The U.S. hits conduct cases and export lists. Europe hits questionnaires, merger jurisdiction, and a statement of objections not yet issued. China hits security reviews and a domestic-substitution list. Three toolkits push on the same physical stack. The resultant is not "more competition." It is "more partition."

One more power sits on the model player and is often dropped from the list. Added analysis. NIM and Nemotron let the same company sell the shovel and open a service on the mine others dig with that shovel. A forced-open CUDA would move the referee and also the model player's distribution channel. Fine only the athlete's sales contracts and the model player is still there. The discussion asked this volume to name the conflict. Name it as far as structure. With no split revenue, there is no "models are already a second profit pool." Hardware is still the bulk. FY26 data center $193.7B against full-year revenue $215.938B is the athlete's size, not the model player's.

Do not write buyer-side power as a fourth corner. Clouds are a channel, ASIC designers, and the profit center of advertising and office suites. They can afford prepayments. They cannot afford the 71% to 75% cabinet-selling margin. Their hundred billion builds halls. NVIDIA's hundred billion locks the next slice of material. Comparing sticker prices is meaningless. If regulation writes the clouds as victims, it misses that the clouds themselves are a gate. If it writes them as conspirators, it needs contracts and mail. This volume has no such docket. What can be written is structure: a buyer oligopoly and a seller's four corners bid for the same slice of CoWoS and HBM. The seller can outbid. The buyer builds halls.

## 8. Close

What Volume 11 should remember is not a fine amount. It is three sentences already nailed. The inquiries are real; complaints and fines have not landed. 10% is a statutory ceiling. FY26 revenue $215,938 million times 10% equals $21,593.8 million, about $21.6B. That is not a number about to be issued. What the national-security files explain is why an inquiry can drag and a split is hard to land. FY26 data center $193.7B is the rent end. Four-cloud mixed-year 2024 about $211.9B to $213.9B, 2025 about $354.0B, is group-wide equipment purchases, not "AI hardware $215B."

The four corners sit in one legal person. The athlete sells cabinets. The referee sets the interface. The coach queues packaging and HBM. The model player stands on the same stack. Regulation hits contracts. It does not hit the queue. A fine does not change the bid. What can change the rules overnight is still a split, forced interoperability, or a ban on tied supply. None of the five interrupts is done. Fail them and the line walks past half of the scarce slice. The world does not blow up. Upgrades stop one node down. Phones and PCs pay a memory tax. One firm writes the rules.

A reader of this volume should not expect a global unified "AI antitrust charter." What is more likely: questionnaires, an inquiry near the end, a merger cleared unconditionally, supply commitments to $119.0B, and a statement of objections not yet issued. Leave empty what is empty. Write a complaint when there is a complaint. Write a fine when there is a fine. If there is none, do not write the ceiling as an invoice.

## 9. Procedure in three countries: if it sits on a step, do not write the next step

Added analysis. Antitrust news most easily writes "near" as "already." The [Volume 11](vol-11.md) master table exists to block that step. The U.S. sits on an inquiry. France sits on an inquiry near the end, not yet deciding whether to issue a statement of objections. The EU sits on questionnaires and one unconditionally approved merger, plus one jurisdiction suit. None of the three steps has reached "complaint / charge / fine." Write any step forward as already happened and this volume is void.

On the U.S. side, the public timeline has only four cells. August 2024: Reuters citing The Information, DOJ already inquiring into tying and networking markups. DOJ declined to comment to AP. 3 September 2024: Bloomberg wrote subpoenas; Bloomberg Law added "a step closer to a formal complaint." 4 September 2024: the company said it had not received a subpoena. 2025 through 26 August 2026: no NVIDIA complaint and no settlement order on justice.gov. A third-party docket itself wrote, as of April 2026, Active DOJ Investigation, no formal complaint, no settlement on record. That is an aggregator, not DOJ. It is only a check that the public record is still empty. A congressional letter on the Groq license does not change this cell. A civil inquiry can run for years. The Sherman Act wants conduct evidence, not a photograph of the gross margin. FY26 gross margin 71.1%, Q4 75.0%, can prove the seller is fat. It cannot replace contracts and mail.

On the French side, the company itself wrote the inquiry into the November 2023 quarterly. A raid is an investigative tool. Berkani's sentence on 9 July 2026 is nearing the end of the investigation; Reuters immediately wrote: The end of the investigation does not mean Nvidia will necessarily face sanctions. The next step is whether to issue a communication des griefs. If not issued, the case can close. Global Competition Review the same day wrote approaching a decision on whether or not to formally accuse. Whether to accuse is still a binary. Writing that sentence as "about to issue a charge" is an add. L464-2's 10% is not usable at this step. No statement of objections, no penalty decision, and therefore no multiplication against worldwide pre-tax turnover.

On the EU side, split it into three different things. First: questionnaires from 2023, tying asked in December 2024. A questionnaire that "could lead to" a formal investigation is not an opening. Second: Run:ai, unconditionally approved on 20 December 2024. Third: T-15/25, the company suing the Commission for expanding merger review under Article 22; Reuters wrote that it does not affect the already-approved deal. None of the three is a 101/102 Opening of proceedings, let alone a statement of objections or a fine. Article 23's 10% and Article 7's structural remedy in Regulation 1/2003 still sit on the power list. Vestager saying chip supply is a bottleneck is a judgment on supply, not a regulatory action. Capitol Forum in February 2026 saying they were still asking how to define data-center components is still news, not an opening.

The three countries sit on different stairs and point at the same judgment sentence: what is done is the inquiry and near-the-end; no complaint, no statement of objections, no 101/102 opening. The window is still there. The window is not "regulators about to move." It is "the few years before the next first-to-market node is locked by $119.0B of commitments." The regulatory calendar and the packaging calendar are not the same table. When C.C. Wei says packaging already limits customer growth, DOJ still has no complaint. The two tables do not line up. That is regulation not hitting the queue.

## 10. Prepayments, rent, and the old 1999 table

The 1999 check and supply commitments are in [Volume 1](vol-01.md). Rent scope is in [Volume 8](vol-08.md). This volume does not reopen the full table.

10-K language: prepaid manufacturing and capacity agreements. Supply commitments $95.2B at FY26 year-end, $119.0B at Q1 FY27 end, up $23.8B in three months, most payable through FY2027. Official text locks inventory and capacity. It does not split TSMC or CoWoS. Some agreements can be rescheduled. Added analysis. If antitrust only hits the tying sentence in the sales contract, it does not hit these commitments. The commitments are to foundry and makers, not a price list to downstream clouds. A forced-open CUDA also does not change this commitment table's due dates. What would change it is a ban on using commitments to punish people who switch stacks, or writing the commitments themselves as exclusive. That takes contract text. This volume has no contract text. So it stops at the mechanism: prepayments buy the slot. A neutrality pledge has no consideration in front of a check. This is not an administrative takeover.

On the rent end, $193.7B is NVIDIA data-center segment full-year revenue. Four-cloud 2025 mixed-year equipment purchases $354,009 million. The two lines cannot be knocked into a rate. Buyer line items include civil works, power, in-house ASICs, memory, and optics. TrendForce puts 47% of 2026 CSP capex and 68% of 2027 into memory. The rising memory share means a slice of the rent lands on HBM and RDIMM, not only on the GPU invoice. Phones and PCs that never buy a training cabinet still pay this slice. HBM+RDIMM is 51% of 2026 DRAM bits. After makers convert, consumer bits shrink. Channel talk: 16G DDR5 from about 450 to 1,800. Apple and Qualcomm pay a spillover price on every consumer bit. That is memory contagion, not a check they wrote to NVIDIA. Huawei's high price is a rent unpaid to NVIDIA inside the iron curtain. Allocation stops domestic bits from leaking out. It does not mean CXMT has been nursed to HBM.

The gap between the 1999 table and today's table is this volume's limit. A share monopoly can be broken by the next slice of capacity. A margin monopoly plus prepayments buys the next slice first. Antitrust habitually hits share and price. Prepayments are a bid for future slots. National-security files then write the same slots as license items. Three powers stack: whoever defines the system, whoever allocates the molecules, whoever approves the electrons. Courts know the first power. The second sits on the boards of TSMC and the three memory firms. The third sits in White House, Commerce, and DoD memoranda. A fine enters the first power's cost line. A split moves the first power and the referee. A forced-open CUDA moves the referee and the model player. A ban on tied supply moves the athlete's contract. None of them automatically moves CoWoS fixture-hours. That is the institutional meaning of the five characters "regulatory limit."

Back to the question. What classic antitrust can still split is contracts and interfaces. What it cannot split is the packaging queue, the HBM conversion, and advanced clusters already written as national security. The inquiry can continue. France can issue a statement of objections, or close. The EU can open, or keep asking how to define components. Until those things happen, this volume leaves them empty. Empty is not unfinished. Empty is the hard rule in [Volume 11](vol-11.md).
